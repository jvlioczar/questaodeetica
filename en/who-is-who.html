Who's who

The authors and institutions cited in the collection: who they are and what they argue in the context of the discussion. It helps you see why each one is mentioned.

Context, not endorsement: many authors are cited precisely as examples of positions the collection criticizes. Each description sums up the position relevant to the debate, not the person's entire body of work. The numbers indicate volume and chapter.

Deny or reduce consideration for animals

Lawrence Becker

American philosopher.

In The Priority of Human Interests (1983) he defends the priority of human interests on the basis of social ties and closeness.

Cited in 1 chapter: I.4

John Benson

British philosopher.

In Duty and the Beast (1978) he accepts duties toward animals, but of lesser weight than duties toward humans.

Cited in 1 chapter: I.1

Peter Carruthers

British-American philosopher, contractualist.

In The Animals Issue (1992) he maintains that animals have no direct moral status, since they cannot take part in a moral contract; our obligations toward them would be only indirect.

Cited in 7 chapters: I.1, I.4, I.12, I.14, II.10, XI.6, XII.1

Ruth Cigman

British philosopher.

In Death, Misfortune and Species Inequality (1981) she argues that death is not a misfortune for animals because they supposedly lack categorical desires about their own future.

Cited in 3 chapters: III.2, III.6, XII.4

Philip Devine

American philosopher.

Discussed the moral basis of vegetarianism and argued that it is reasonable to give more weight to humans.

Cited in 1 chapter: I.5

Cora Diamond

American philosopher in the Wittgensteinian tradition.

In Eating Meat and Eating People (1978) she argues that our morality is shaped by relationships and by the idea of "fellow human beings," not by capacities; she accepts compassion for animals, but not equality.

Cited in 4 chapters: I.1, I.2, I.14, XI.6

Luc Ferry

French philosopher and former government minister.

In The New Ecological Order (1992) he criticizes deep ecology and antispeciesism, defending a humanism in which only humans have full dignity.

Cited in 1 chapter: I.4

R. G. Frey

British-American philosopher (1941–2012).

In Interests and Rights (1980) he denied that animals have interests in the moral sense because they lack language; he defended animal experimentation.

Cited in 1 chapter: I.4

Raimond Gaita

Australian philosopher.

In The Philosopher's Dog (2002) he explores the relationship between humans and animals, defending a view that sets apart the moral place of humans.

Cited in 2 chapters: I.2, I.14

Michael Leahy

British philosopher.

In Against Liberation (1991), drawing on Wittgenstein, he denies that animals have a mental life rich enough to carry the same moral weight as humans.

Cited in 3 chapters: I.4, I.5, XI.6

Tibor Machan

Hungarian-American libertarian philosopher (1939–2016).

In Putting Humans First (2004) he argues that humans have moral priority because they are moral agents, and that animals have no rights.

Cited in 3 chapters: I.6, XI.6, XII.1

H. J. McCloskey

Australian philosopher.

Argued that only those who can claim something for themselves can have rights, which would exclude animals.

Cited in 1 chapter: I.5

Mary Midgley

British philosopher (1919–2018).

In Animals and Why They Matter (1983) she criticizes the treatment of animals but argues that preferring one's own species is a natural bond, and to some extent a legitimate one.

Cited in 1 chapter: I.4

Jan Narveson

Canadian philosopher, libertarian contractualist.

Argues that morality is an agreement among those who can cooperate and retaliate; since animals cannot, they would have no rights (Animal Rights, 1977).

Cited in 3 chapters: I.1, I.4, I.12

William Paton

British pharmacologist (1917–1993).

In Man and Mouse (1984) he defends animal experimentation for its medical benefits to humans.

Cited in 1 chapter: I.5

Lewis Petrinovich

American evolutionary psychologist.

In Darwinian Dominion (1999) he argues that favoring one's own species is natural and morally justified by evolution.

Cited in 2 chapters: I.4, I.12

Richard Posner

American judge and legal theorist.

In a debate with Singer, he argued that it is legitimate to give preference to humans and to trust the intuition that humans matter more.

Cited in 2 chapters: I.2, I.14

Roger Scruton

British conservative philosopher (1944–2020).

In Animal Rights and Wrongs (1996) he claims that rights belong only to members of the moral community, who are capable of duties; to animals we owe only a certain kindness.

Cited in 4 chapters: I.6, III.8, IV.7, XII.1

Scott Wilson

American philosopher.

Argues that human status depends on the "species norm" (The Species-Norm Account of Human Status, 2005).

Cited in 2 chapters: I.1, II.2

Environmental ethics and holism

Bernice Bovenkerk

Dutch philosopher (Wageningen).

Works at the intersection of animal ethics and environmental ethics, including the management of wild animals.

Cited in 5 chapters: VII.13, VII.21, VII.22, VII.26, XII.6

J. Baird Callicott

American environmental philosopher, the leading interpreter of the "land ethic."

Defends a holistic ethics: the good of the biotic community (ecosystems and species) is the ultimate criterion of value. In Animal Liberation: A Triangular Affair (1980) he went as far as to claim that this good can outweigh that of individuals, humans included; he later softened his position.

Cited in 14 chapters: I.1, I.4, IV.15, V.1, V.2, V.7, V.8, VII.16, VII.20, XI.6, XII.1, XII.3, XII.5, XII.6

Robert Elliot

Australian philosopher.

In Faking Nature (1982) he argues that original nature has a value that restored nature does not recover.

Cited in 2 chapters: V.1, VII.20

Kenneth Goodpaster

American philosopher.

In On Being Morally Considerable (1978) he argues that being alive, not being sentient, is the criterion for moral consideration.

Cited in 4 chapters: V.1, VI.15, VI.17, VII.20

Eugene Hargrove

American environmental philosopher.

Grounds the value of nature in its aesthetic qualities.

Cited in 3 chapters: V.1, V.3, V.4

Ned Hettinger

American environmental philosopher.

Defends the value of "wild" nature and discusses exotic species and respect for the autonomy of natural processes.

Cited in 5 chapters: IV.15, V.1, V.2, V.7, V.8

Holmes Rolston III

American environmental philosopher.

Attributes objective value to ecosystems and species, and argues that predation is part of the value of nature.

Cited in 8 chapters: IV.15, V.1, V.2, V.7, V.8, VII.23, XII.3, XII.5

Eric Katz

American environmental philosopher.

Criticizes restoration of and intervention in nature as forms of human domination over it.

Cited in 2 chapters: V.1, VII.20

Aldo Leopold

American ecologist and forester (1887–1948).

In A Sand County Almanac (1949) he formulated the land ethic: a thing is right when it tends to preserve the integrity, stability and beauty of the biotic community. The value lies in the whole, not in individual animals.

Cited in 6 chapters: I.1, IV.15, V.1, V.7, V.8, VII.20

Pentti Linkola

Radical Finnish ecologist (1932–2020).

Placed the preservation of nature above the good of humans and other animals, going so far as to advocate a drastic, authoritarian reduction of the human population. He is cited as an extreme example of holistic environmentalism.

Cited in 3 chapters: V.7, V.9, VI.14

Arne Næss

Norwegian philosopher (1912–2009), founder of deep ecology.

Attributes intrinsic value to nature and to the diversity of life as a whole, not only to sentient beings.

Cited in 4 chapters: I.1, I.4, V.7, V.8

Mark Sagoff

American environmental philosopher.

In Animal Liberation and Environmental Ethics: Bad Marriage, Quick Divorce (1984) he argues that animal advocacy and environmentalism are incompatible.

Cited in 1 chapter: IV.15

Albert Schweitzer

Theologian, physician and philosopher (1875–1965).

Proposed "reverence for life": every living being deserves respect, an idea that inspired biocentrism.

Cited in 2 chapters: V.7, VI.17

Jo-Ann Shelton

American researcher.

Analyzes environmentalist programs that kill animals considered "out of place," such as introduced species.

Cited in 5 chapters: V.1, V.8, VI.11, VII.10, XII.5

Michael Soulé

American biologist (1936–2020), one of the founders of conservation biology.

Argued that biological diversity has value in itself and that conservation should protect species and ecosystems.

Cited in 2 chapters: V.6, VII.27

Paul W. Taylor

American philosopher (1923–2015).

In Respect for Nature (1986) he defends biocentrism: every living being has a "good of its own" and inherent value, whether sentient animals, plants or microorganisms.

Cited in 9 chapters: IV.20, V.1, VI.15, VI.17, VI.21, VII.18, VII.20, VII.31, XII.6

Gary Varner

American philosopher.

In In Nature's Interests? (1998) he argues that all living beings have "biological interests," although he gives priority to the interests of conscious beings.

Cited in 3 chapters: V.7, V.8, VI.20

Peter Wenz

American philosopher.

Proposes a "concentric circles" theory: stronger obligations toward those who are closer to us, within a pluralistic environmental ethics.

Cited in 3 chapters: IV.15, V.7, V.8

Defend consideration for sentient beings

Cheryl Abbate

American philosopher.

Applies virtue ethics to the animal question, showing that it too condemns harming animals unnecessarily.

Cited in 2 chapters: XI.5, XI.6

Carol J. Adams

American ecofeminist writer.

In The Sexual Politics of Meat (1990) she links the oppression of women to that of animals; she is associated with the ethics of care.

Cited in 1 chapter: V.7

Paola Cavalieri

Italian philosopher.

In The Animal Question (2001) she extends human rights theory to animals; with Singer, she organized the Great Ape Project.

Cited in 1 chapter: III.4

Luciano Carlos Cunha

Author of this collection, PhD in ethics and political philosophy (UFSC).

In the works cited, he defends equal consideration for all sentient beings, including wild animals who are victims of natural processes.

Cited in 74 chapters: I.1, I.4, I.7, I.9, I.10, I.11, II.1, II.3, II.5, II.6, II.7, II.8, II.10, II.11 and 60 more

David DeGrazia

American philosopher.

In Taking Animals Seriously (1996) he defends serious consideration for animals, but allows different weights depending on mental complexity, especially regarding the harm of death.

Cited in 1 chapter: I.1

Daniel Dombrowski

American philosopher.

In Babies and Beasts (1997) he analyzes the history of the argument from species overlap.

Cited in 2 chapters: XI.5, XI.6

Josephine Donovan

American feminist theorist.

Develops the ethics of care as applied to animals, centered on empathy and attention to suffering.

Cited in 2 chapters: V.7, XI.5

Jessica Ehnert

Philosopher.

Systematized the argument from species overlap (The Argument from Species Overlap, 2002).

Cited in 5 chapters: I.4, I.6, I.10, I.12, II.2

Animal Ethics

International nonprofit organization that funded this collection.

Promotes moral consideration for all sentient beings, focusing on speciesism, wild animals and welfare biology.

Cited in 32 chapters: I.1, II.5, II.13, IV.6, IV.7, VI.10, VI.11, VI.12, VII.3, VII.21, VII.24, VII.26, VII.27, VII.28 and 18 more

Gary L. Francione

American legal scholar and philosopher.

Defends the abolitionist approach: the central problem is that animals are property; he proposes veganism as a moral baseline and criticizes welfarist reforms.

Julian Franklin

American political philosopher (1925–2013).

In Animal Rights and Moral Philosophy (2005) he argues that Kant's categorical imperative, properly understood, requires treating all sentient beings as ends in themselves.

Cited in 5 chapters: I.1, II.1, VI.1, XI.5, XI.6

Lewis Gompertz

British writer and inventor (1783–1861).

A pioneer: in Moral Inquiries (1824) he advocated not using animal products and was already discussing helping animals against natural harms such as predation.

Cited in 2 chapters: XI.5, XI.6

Oscar Horta

Spanish philosopher (University of Santiago de Compostela), cofounder of the organization Animal Ethics.

One of the leading authors on speciesism and on the suffering of wild animals; argues that natural harms matter just as much as those caused by humans.

Cited in 62 chapters: I.1, I.3, I.4, I.6, I.7, I.10, I.11, I.12, I.14, II.1, II.2, II.3, II.5, II.10 and 48 more

Mark Rowlands

British philosopher.

Richard D. Ryder

Peter Singer

In Animal Liberation (1975) he popularized the term "speciesism" and the principle of equal consideration of interests: suffering of equal intensity counts equally, no matter whose it is.

Donald Broom

Animal welfare scientist (Cambridge).

Studies indicators of sentience and of well-being in many species.

Cited in 6 chapters: II.3, II.4, IV.1, VI.1, VI.8, VI.18

Our World in Data

Data publication based at the University of Oxford.

Source of the global statistics on slaughtered animals.

Cited in 3 chapters: VIII.2, VIII.3, X.1

EFSA

European Food Safety Authority.

Has published scientific opinions on the sentience and welfare of animals used in production.

Cited in 3 chapters: II.13, IV.19, VI.6

Gail Eisnitz

American investigator.

In Slaughterhouse (1997) she gathered testimony from slaughterhouse workers about what happens to the animals.

Cited in 1 chapter: IV.1

Fishcount

British research project.

Estimates the number of fish caught and farmed each year worldwide.

Cited in 6 chapters: IV.1, IV.13, VIII.2, VIII.3, X.1, XII.10

Daniel Kahneman

Israeli-American psychologist (1934–2024), Nobel laureate in economics.

In Thinking, Fast and Slow (2011) he describes the biases and heuristics that distort our judgments, the basis of Volume X.

Cited in 7 chapters: III.6, IX.13, X.1, X.2, X.3, X.4, X.6

Jennifer Mather

Canadian comparative psychologist.

Researches cognition and sentience in invertebrates, especially octopuses.

Cited in 6 chapters: II.3, II.4, IV.1, VI.1, VI.6, VI.18

Lynne Sneddon

British biologist.

A pioneering researcher on pain in fish.

Cited in 3 chapters: II.4, IV.1, VII.3

Frans de Waal

Dutch primatologist (1948–2024).

Showed empathy and prosocial behavior in other primates, discussing the origins of morality.

Cited in 1 chapter: I.12

Classics of philosophy

Aristotle

Greek philosopher (384–322 BCE).

Founder of virtue ethics; he saw animals as existing for human use, within a natural hierarchy.

Cited in 2 chapters: X.2, XI.5

Jeremy Bentham

British philosopher (1748–1832), founder of utilitarianism.

Wrote that the question is not whether animals can reason or talk, but whether they can suffer.

Cited in 4 chapters: II.2, II.3, VI.1, XI.7

Epicurus

Greek philosopher (341–270 BCE).

Held that death is not an evil: "where death is, we are not." The collection discusses and rejects this view.

Cited in 1 chapter: III.1

Thomas Hobbes

English philosopher (1588–1679).

A contractualist: morality arises from an agreement among self-interested people to avoid the war of all against all.

Cited in 1 chapter: XI.6

Immanuel Kant

German philosopher (1724–1804), author of the ethics of the categorical imperative.

Held that only rational beings are ends in themselves; our duties regarding animals would be indirect (cruelty to them would make us cruel to humans). Contemporary Kantians such as Korsgaard argue that the theory itself implies considering animals directly.

Cited in 4 chapters: I.1, II.1, VI.1, XI.6

John Locke

English philosopher (1632–1704).

Defined a person by a consciousness that extends over time, an idea used in the debate on identity and the harm of death.

Cited in 2 chapters: III.8, IV.7

Lucretius

Roman Epicurean poet and philosopher (1st century BCE).

Formulated the symmetry argument: we do not regret the time before we were born, so we should not regret the time after death.

Cited in 1 chapter: III.3

John Stuart Mill

British philosopher (1806–1873), utilitarian.

In the essay "Nature" he shows that "natural" does not mean "good": nature routinely does things we would condemn in humans.

Cited in 6 chapters: I.8, III.4, IV.19, VII.31, VIII.12, XI.7

Plato

Greek philosopher (c. 428–348 BCE).

In the dialogue Euthyphro he formulated the dilemma: is something good because the gods approve of it, or do the gods approve of it because it is good?

Cited in 1 chapter: I.13
