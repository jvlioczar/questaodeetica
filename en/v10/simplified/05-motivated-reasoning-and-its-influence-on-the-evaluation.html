Motivated Reasoning and Its Influence on the Evaluation of Arguments

In plain language, for readers who are new to the topic. About 2 min of reading.

What is this page? A simplified explanation written for this site based on Luciano Carlos Cunha’s text. It leaves out details, examples and sources. For the full argument, read the author’s original text.

On this page

  1. Double standards when judging arguments
  2. Different levels of strictness
  3. When people don't believe what they say
  4. The availability cascade
  5. The consequence
  6. In short

Double standards when judging arguments

In almost every debate about animals, certain claims come up again and again, like "animals don't feel the way we do" or "it's natural." These claims reveal an important bias: motivated reasoning.

New word

Motivated reasoning: thinking in a way that confirms what we already wanted to believe, instead of looking for the truth.

Different levels of strictness

Because of motivated reasoning and confirmation bias, we judge arguments that agree with what we already think much less strictly, and arguments that disagree much more strictly.

This bias affects everyone, including philosophers who are experts in ethics.

When people don't believe what they say

There is a possibility that almost never gets considered: sometimes the person doesn't believe the justification they give. They use it as an excuse. This can happen in three ways:

  • self-deception: the person tries to fool themselves;
  • fooling the other person: the person thinks the listener will accept it, or will have a hard time refuting it;
  • mutual pretending: everyone can tell the argument is weak, but since they agree with the conclusion, they pretend it's good.

The third case is very common.

The availability cascade

A shared belief can gain more and more acceptance just because it's repeated many times, even if it makes no sense.

The consequence

This helps explain why attempts to justify speciesism, and objections to helping wild animals, are rarely examined carefully, even in academia.

Remember

To think well, we need to judge the arguments we like and the ones we don't with the same strictness.

In short

  • Motivated reasoning makes us look for confirmation, not the truth.
  • Sometimes people use arguments they don't even believe.
  • Repeating an idea many times doesn't make it true.
