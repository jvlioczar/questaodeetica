The Appeal to Differentiated Degrees of Moral Status

In plain language, for readers who are new to the topic. About 3 min of reading.

What is this page? A simplified explanation written for this site based on Luciano Carlos Cunha’s text. It leaves out details, examples and sources. For the full argument, read the author’s original text.

On this page

  1. Two ways to decide
  2. Versions of special status
  3. How to tell if this is fair: the veil of ignorance
  4. What about those who don't understand justice?
  5. In short

Two ways to decide

Imagine you can only help one of two individuals. How do you choose? Let's compare two ideas.

Magnitude Thesis: help whoever will be harmed more if they're not helped. What counts is the size of the harm, not the species.

Special Status Thesis: some individuals have a "special status," like a wild card. They should come first even when their harm is smaller.

New word

Moral status: the "weight" someone has in ethical decisions.

Versions of special status

  • Full version: whoever has special status always comes first, no matter how much the other one suffers.
  • Version with a limit: whoever has special status comes first, unless the other one's suffering goes past a certain point.

In both versions, you sometimes have to help the one who would suffer less. Special status is usually given to humans, or to the smartest beings.

How to tell if this is fair: the veil of ignorance

A famous test, inspired by the philosophers John Harsanyi and John Rawls, helps answer this. Imagine you're about to be born, but you don't know who you'll be. You could be anyone affected by a rule: a human, a pig, a chicken.

Now choose the rules of the world. The idea is: whatever you'd choose without knowing who you'll be is what's fair.

New word

Veil of ignorance: imagining you don't know which place you'll end up in, so you decide without favoring yourself.

Without knowing who you'd be, would you accept a rule that puts a small pain of a "special" being above a huge pain of your own? Probably not. You'd want the size of the suffering to count.

What about those who don't understand justice?

Some people say this test only applies to those who have a sense of justice. The answer is that if that were true, babies and children would be left out too, and that's not acceptable.

Remember

When nobody knows which place they'll end up in, the rule that seems fair is to give more weight to whoever suffers more, whatever their species.

In short

  • The Magnitude Thesis looks at the size of the harm.
  • The Special Status Thesis gives priority to some, even when they suffer less.
  • In the veil of ignorance test, the Magnitude Thesis is the fair choice.
