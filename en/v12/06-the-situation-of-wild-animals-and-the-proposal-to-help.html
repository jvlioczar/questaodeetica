Before you read study layer, not part of the original text

The proposal to help wild animals rests on the idea that the strength of our reasons depends on the magnitude of the harm, not its origin; and it answers the objections that wild animals live well, that destroyed eggs reduce the number of victims, and that newborns barely suffer.

Animals in the wild suffer a lot from hunger, cold, and disease, and most of them die as babies. If we care about animals, it does not matter whether the suffering is caused by a person or by nature.

For someone who is drowning, it makes no difference whether they were pushed or slipped into the water: they need help just the same.

  1. P1 Our reasons to prevent harm depend on its magnitude, not on whether its origin is human or natural.
  2. P2 Animals in the wild suffer enormous harms, and most are born only to die young.
  3. P3 Even discounting destroyed eggs, the number of victims is gigantic.
  4. C So the situation of wild animals is as important as it would be if the harms were caused by humans.

The chapter’s central argument in logical form. If the premises are true, the conclusion follows. To disagree, you have to show which premise is false.

They say “Wild animals live well if we leave them alone.”

Answer Malnutrition, disease, parasites, weather, and predation are the norm. With litters in the thousands, almost all the young die early.

Original text Luciano Carlos Cunha · Volume XII, p. 21 of the PDF edition · English translation

As we saw in the previous section, most programs dealing with are driven by ideals. In these programs animals are not valued in themselves, but only as a means to preserve species and ecosystems. In recent decades, however, several authors22 have argued that programs dealing with wild animals should have as their goal the good of the animals themselves, seeking to prevent and minimize their suffering and deaths.

Since the emergence of animal ethics, whether with respect to domesticated or wild animals, the focus has generally been on animals harmed by human practices. However, a growing number of authors23 have drawn attention to the way animals are harmed by , especially animals living in the wild, and have proposed researching ways to reduce these harms. The central argument of this proposal is that, if we really care about animals, we will not limit our concern to harms caused by humans, since for the animals it makes no difference whether what harms them is a human or, say, hunger or disease. In more detail, the central argument is as follows24: if what gives us reasons to avoid harms is the fact that they are something negative, and if what gives us reasons to pursue benefits is the fact that they are something positive, then the strength of these reasons depends on the magnitude of the harms and benefits at stake, and not on whether their origin is anthropogenic or natural. In other words, we have reasons to give priority to avoiding the greater harm and to pursuing the greater benefit, regardless of whether they originate in human practices or in natural processes. Thus, the situation of wild animals who are victims of natural processes is just as important as it would be if these were equivalent anthropogenic harms.

In this section and the next two we will look at the debate on the proposal to reduce the suffering and deaths of wild animals25. In this section we will discuss the objections26 claiming that there is no need to help them, and the answers that have been given to these objections.

Objection How the objection has been answered
There is no need to help animals in the wild: it is enough to stop harming them, since, if we let nature take its course, animals will generally have predominantly positive lives. (VII, ch. 3).Animals in the wild are greatly harmed by natural processes. Malnutrition, hunger, thirst, disease, physical injuries, psychological stress, hostile weather events, natural disasters, and various kinds of conflict between animals are the norm in nature. In addition, the vast majority of animal species reproduce by maximizing offspring (in broods ranging from thousands to many millions, depending on the species). In periods of approximately stable population, it can be deduced that, on average, in each generation a single offspring per adult survives (that is, only two per brood). All the rest of the brood are generally born only to experience extreme suffering and die very 27 prematurely. Because of these factors, the number of animals who suffer and die as a result of natural processes is so enormous that it makes even the numbers of animal exploitation look small by comparison28.
Many eggs are destroyed before they develop into sentient beings. Thus, few animals are harmed. (VII, ch. 3).Even if this happened to the vast majority of eggs, there would still be an enormous number of individuals born to live a life in which suffering greatly predominates. For example, even if 90% of the 2 million eggs laid by a cod were destroyed before developing into sentient beings, the remaining 10% would still be 200,000 individuals.
Most animals die very young, and at that stage they are still barely capable of suffering. Therefore, they are only slightly harmed by what happens to them. (VII, ch. 3).There is no evidence that, because they are very young, they are barely capable of suffering. On the contrary, given that most of these animals are precocial, it is quite likely that their capacity to feel is very acute at this stage, since otherwise 29 such species would probably no longer exist. And even if they were barely capable of suffering, this does not mean they were only slightly unfortunate, since premature death aggravates the harm of death (they lived short lives full of suffering).
