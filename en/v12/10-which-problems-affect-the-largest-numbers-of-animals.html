Before you read study layer, not part of the original text

The 80 billion animals killed per year that activists cite are only mammals and birds, between 0.1% and 0.2% of the 34 to 54 trillion killed in exploitation; and animals in the wild, numbering 1 to 10 quintillion, make even these figures look small.

If we squeezed all the animals into a single calendar year, the animals raised for exploitation would take up only a few seconds. The rest of the whole year would be animals in the wild. There are so many of them, truly so many.

If the entire animal population were one year, exploited animals would be at most 14 seconds, and wild animals would be all the rest of the year.

  1. P1 Most campaigns cite 80 billion animals killed per year.
  2. P2 Adding fish, crustaceans, insects, and others, the figure is between 34 and 54 trillion.
  3. P3 Sentient animals in the wild are estimated at 1 to 10 quintillion at any given moment.
  4. C So the problems that affect the most animals are precisely the least remembered.

The chapter’s central argument in logical form. If the premises are true, the conclusion follows. To disagree, you have to show which premise is false.

They say “About 80 billion animals are killed for food each year.”

Answer That number includes only mammals and birds. With fish, shrimp, insects, and cochineal insects, the estimate is hundreds of times larger.

Below, we will look at the problems that affect the largest numbers of animals34

10.1. Exploited animals

Animal advocates, wishing to show how large the number of animals killed for consumption is, often say that around 80 billion animals are killed worldwide each year. However, this figure refers only to mammals and birds. The total number of animals killed is vastly larger. Below are some data on annual deaths of exploited animals at the global level.

Type of exploited animal Animals killed worldwide per year
Mammals and birds35Around 80 billion
Factory-farmed fishBetween 51 and 167 billion36.
Fish caught directly at seaBetween 787 billion and 2.3 trillion37.
Aquatic animals used as feed for fish farmed for consumption38Between 462 billion and 1.1 trillion.
Factory-farmed crustaceansBetween 255 and 604 billion39.
Silkworms for silk production40Between 420 billion and 1 trillion
Insects killed for consumption41Between 2 and 3.2 trillion
Cochineal insects in the production of carmine dyeBetween 4.6 and 21 trillion42.
Shrimp caught directly at seaAround 25 trillion43

The sum of these numbers suggests that somewhere between 34 and 54 trillion animals are killed annually at the global level. This means that the 80 billion usually mentioned by activists represent only somewhere between 0.1% and 0.2% of the animals killed in exploitation.

10.2. Animals in the wild

There are no statistics on the number of animals in the wild who die each year as a result of . But to get an idea of the difference in proportion, we can make a comparison with the populations at a given moment.

  • The total population of animals in the wild at a given moment would be, according to certain estimates, between 1 and 10 quintillion individuals44.
  • Let us compare this, for example, with the world population at a given moment of animals bred as stock to be exploited (a total population of 434 billion):
  • Terrestrial vertebrates: 24 billion45.
  • Fish: 180 billion46.
  • Shrimp: 230 billion47.
  • Total: 434 billion.

How much larger is 1 to 10 quintillion compared to 434 billion? These numbers are so enormous that it is hard to visualize the difference. So, to understand it, it is useful to make an analogy with the span of a year. The combined populations of animals bred to be exploited would represent at most 14 seconds of the year. All the rest of the year (364 days, 23 hours, 59 minutes, and 46 seconds) would be the animals in the wild. This gives us a rough sense of how large the number of animals in the wild is. As we have seen, the vast majority of these animals have lives in which suffering greatly predominates.

10.3. The future

These numbers, both of animals harmed by human practices and of those harmed by natural processes, may become astronomically larger in the future. That is why, in recent years, the debate on the importance of how our decisions affect the long-term future has been growing, as we will see in the next chapter.
