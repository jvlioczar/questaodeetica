Before you read study layer, not part of the original text

Respecting sentient beings doesn't imply that nature has intrinsic value: what makes sentient beings considerable (being able to be affected) isn't present in nature as a whole; confusing “nature” with “animals” produces this mistake.

Animals are part of nature, but “nature” isn't an animal. It doesn't feel. Liking animals doesn't mean you have to think all of nature has feelings.

The players are part of the team, but protecting the players' health isn't the same as protecting “the team” as a brand.

  1. P1 The argument says: if sentient beings should be respected and are part of nature, nature has intrinsic value.
  2. P2 Sentient beings are considered because they can be affected positively or negatively.
  3. P3 Nature as a whole doesn't have this feature.
  4. C Therefore, considering sentient beings doesn't imply giving intrinsic value to nature.

The chapter’s central argument in logical form. If the premises are true, the conclusion follows. To disagree, you have to show which premise is false.

They say “If you defend animals, you have to defend all of nature.”

Answer One doesn't imply the other. It's this confusion between “nature” and “animals” that makes it seem that preserving nature is always good for animals.

More objections and answers in Debunking objections.

Original text Luciano Carlos Cunha · Volume VI, p. 34 of the PDF edition · English translation

It is sometimes argued that if beings should receive , then nature has value in itself, since sentient beings are part of nature. On this view, this would also show that if we deny that nature has , we are at the same time disregarding sentient beings.

This view is sometimes raised as an objection to the proposal to help wild animals that are victims of natural processes41. This proposal involves intervening in such as hunger, thirst, disease, and natural disasters in order to benefit animals42. The objection then claims that if we hold that it is right to intervene in nature, we must also hold that it is right to intervene in sentient beings, even to harm them, since they are part of nature.

The premise underlying this objection is that if a part of something is morally considerable, then the whole of that something is as well. However, there is no necessary implication from a part being morally considerable to the whole of which it is a part also being so. This is because the reasons for considering the part may not be present in the rest of the group to which it belongs, nor in the whole.

This is exactly what happens in the case of sentient beings: the reason we consider them is that they can be affected positively or negatively (because they are able to experience certain states as positive and/or others as negative). This characteristic is not present in any other part of nature, nor in nature as a whole. Therefore, the fact that there are good reasons to consider sentient beings does not imply that there are good reasons to consider other parts of nature, or nature as a whole.

It is therefore false that, if we hold that it is right to modify nature, we will be authorizing harm to nonhuman animals because they are part of nature. This is safely avoided if we make clear that the prescription is: nonsentient nature should be modified in order to benefit sentient beings.

It is precisely the confusion of thinking that “nature” and “nonhuman animals” are the same thing that gives rise to the (completely mistaken) idea that views that advocate preserving nature as a whole necessarily also take nonhuman animals into consideration43. This same confusion in the use of terms also seems to be what leads many people to approve of any and every environmentalist policy, mistakenly believing that they are supporting policies that favor nonhuman animals44.
