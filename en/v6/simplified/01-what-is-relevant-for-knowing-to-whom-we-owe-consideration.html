What is relevant for knowing to whom we owe consideration?

In plain language, for readers who are new to the topic. About 2 min of reading.

What is this page? A simplified explanation written for this site based on Luciano Carlos Cunha’s text. It leaves out details, examples and sources. For the full argument, read the author’s original text.

A volume about the sentience standard

The central idea is this: we owe moral consideration to all sentient beings, meaning everyone who can feel. And the most common criticisms of this idea have answers. The first chapter goes back over why sentience is the right standard.

The double standard

Humans exploit animals every day and hardly care about the suffering of wild animals. If the victims were human, the reaction would be the opposite. It's a case of a double standard.

The excuses and the question that knocks them down

To justify this, people have said humans matter more because they're human, have a soul, reason, language, culture, social bonds, or because it's natural or traditional. For each excuse, you can ask: what does this have to do with being able to be harmed?

None of these things decides whether someone can suffer or be happy.

The argument from relevance

  • To know who we owe consideration to, what matters is who can be harmed or helped.
  • Those who can be harmed or helped are sentient beings.
  • So we owe consideration to all sentient beings, of any species.

New word

Standard for moral consideration: the rule that says who we should take into account when we decide what to do.

Remember

The right standard is one that has to do with what's at stake. If what's at stake is getting hurt, the standard is being able to feel.

In short

  • All the excuses for ignoring animals fail at the same point.
  • What matters is being able to be harmed or helped.
  • So the standard is sentience.
