Volume VI · Intermediate level

To Whom Do We Owe Moral Consideration?

The debate on the sentience criterion

21 chaptersabout 72 min of reading73 notes93 references

0 of 21 read

About this volume

This book presents the grounds for choosing sentience as the criterion for moral consideration, as well as the criticisms that have been made of this criterion and the responses that have been offered to these criticisms.

ISBN 978-65-01-44289-1 · 1st edition, April 2025. Original text by Luciano Carlos Cunha, translated into English.

In a nutshell

The debate on the sentience criterion: some twenty criticisms (anthropocentrism, discrimination between kingdoms, harms without suffering, insects, subjectivity, degrees of sentience, biocentrism, the value of life, biological interests, plants) and the replies its defenders give.

For kids

A rock does not feel anything, so you cannot hurt a rock. A plant is alive, but as far as we know it does not feel. A fish does feel. This book explains why feeling is what makes it possible for someone to be hurt.

You will learn

  • The main criticisms of the sentience criterion and the replies to them
  • Why the criterion is not anthropocentric and does not exclude invertebrates
  • Why being alive, on its own, is not enough to be harmed
  • What would follow if plants were sentient

It helps to have read: Volume II.

Chapters

  1. 1
    What is relevant for knowing to whom we owe consideration?

    A criterion is justified only if it's based on a distinction relevant to the question at hand; to decide whom to give consideration, what's relevant is being able to be harmed or benefited, which leads to the criterion of sentience.

    9 min
  2. 2
    Is the criterion of sentience anthropocentric at bottom?

    The sentience criterion isn't anthropocentric: it doesn't consider animals because they resemble humans, but because they can be harmed and benefited, which reason or language doesn't determine.

    1 min
  3. 3
    Does the criterion of sentience discriminate against those who do not belong to the animal kingdom?

    The sentience criterion isn't “kingdomism”: it doesn't include all animals (sponges probably don't feel) or exclude other kingdoms on principle; it includes any sentient being, even a non-organic one, should one come to exist.

    2 min
  4. 4
    According to the criterion of sentience, is suffering the only problem?

    The sentience criterion doesn't recognize only suffering as harm: a sentient being is also harmed by the absence of what would be positive for them, as in death.

    2 min
  5. 5
    If sentience is what matters, why are there harms that do not affect experiences?

    Harms that don't alter one's experiences, such as violating someone who is unconscious, may be an objection to hedonism, but not to the sentience criterion, which is also compatible with harms to actual or informed preferences.

    3 min
  6. 6
    Does the criterion of sentience exclude insects?

    The sentience criterion doesn't exclude insects: there's evidence of sentience in several species, and the rest have barely been studied; if there's someone in those bodies, they deserve the same consideration as any sentient being.

    4 min
  7. 7
    Does accepting the criterion of sentience imply assuming that values are subjective?

    Accepting the sentience criterion doesn't imply that values are subjective: there's a confusion between “subjective” in the sense of referring to consciousness and in the sense of there being no objective truth.

    2 min
  8. 8
    Are animals with simpler brains less sentient?

    There are no good reasons to say that animals with simpler brains are less sentient or deserve less: they're as much a “someone” as any sentient being, their experiences may even be more intense at certain stages, and giving less weight because of cognition is intelligence-based discrimination.

    8 min
  9. 9
    If there is a need for environmental ethics, is the criterion of sentience wrong?

    The existence of an environmental ethics doesn't show that non-sentient entities have intrinsic value: we also need an ethics of income distribution, and income has no intrinsic value. An “ethics of environmental management” may be enough.

    3 min
  10. 10
    Isn't a World with Less Suffering, but Managed by Humans, Absurd?

    The idea that a world with less suffering but managed by humans would be worse reveals a prejudice: an ecosystem indistinguishable from a natural one and better for sentient beings wouldn't be worse just because of human influence.

    3 min
  11. 11
    If Sentient Beings Should Be Respected, Does Nature Have Intrinsic Value?

    Respecting sentient beings doesn't imply that nature has intrinsic value: what makes sentient beings considerable (being able to be affected) isn't present in nature as a whole; confusing “nature” with “animals” produces this mistake.

    2 min
  12. 12
    Does the Sentience Criterion Imply Accepting Environmentalist Goals?

    The fact that some sentient beings have an interest in nature taking its course doesn't imply accepting environmentalist goals: considering an interest isn't the same as having to fulfill it, and fulfilling it here would cause suffering and death to an enormous number of others.

    2 min
  13. 13
    Do Species Have Interests?

    Species don't have interests: they're abstract entities, categories created to group similar living beings; those who can be benefited or harmed are the sentient individuals who make them up.

    4 min
  14. 14
    If Some Nonsentient Entity Had Intrinsic Value, What Would Follow?

    Even if non-sentient entities had intrinsic value, there would be strong reasons to prioritize sentient beings in conflicts: only they can suffer, and only for them does death prevent positive experiences.

    3 min
  15. 15
    Is Biocentrism Better Because It Includes a Greater Number of Beings?

    A broader criterion isn't necessarily better: it would have to be shown that the beings included can actually be harmed; and biocentrism excludes possible non-living sentient beings, discriminating against them.

    3 min
  16. 16
    Sentience and Life: What Has Value as an End and What Has Value as a Means?

    That being alive is necessary for being sentient doesn't give life intrinsic value; it's the other way around: biological life has value as a means to meaningful experiences, not sentience as a means to life.

    4 min
  17. 17
    Can Nonsentient Living Beings Be Harmed?

    Non-sentient living beings don't seem capable of being harmed: everyday expressions like “the plant is happy” don't show that there's someone there for whom things go well or badly.

    3 min
  18. 18
    Does the Behavior of Certain Plants and Fungi Show That They Value Certain Things?

    Behaviors of plants and fungi (growing toward light, closing leaves when touched) can be explained as physicochemical reactions; without a structure that gives rise to experiences, behavior alone doesn't indicate sentience.

    5 min
  19. 19
    Does Life Without Sentience Have Value?

    Life without sentience doesn't seem to have value: examples like total irreversible coma, the fraudulent insurer, and sentience without life show that what we value is experiences, not biological life.

    4 min
  20. 20
    Does Every Living Being Have “Biological Interests”?

    Non-sentient living beings don't have “biological interests” in the moral sense: in sentient beings, satisfying biological needs is only a means to well-being; having needs isn't enough to have interests.

    3 min
  21. 21
    If Plants Are Morally Considerable, Does It Make No Difference Whether We Eat Animals or Plants?

    If plants were morally considerable, it wouldn't follow that it makes no difference whether we eat animals or plants: animals suffer and consume many plants; the implication would be fruitarianism, and even Paul Taylor recognizes the importance of suffering.

    2 min
