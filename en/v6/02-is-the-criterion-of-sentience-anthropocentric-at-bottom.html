Before you read study layer, not part of the original text

The sentience criterion isn't anthropocentric: it doesn't consider animals because they resemble humans, but because they can be harmed and benefited, which reason or language doesn't determine.

Some people say caring about those who feel is just caring about those who are like us. But that's not it: the reason to care for someone who feels is that they can be hurt, not that they seem human.

A firefighter rescues people and cats from a fire not because cats look like people, but because both can get burned.

  1. P1 Anthropocentrism assigns value to animals according to how much they resemble humans.
  2. P2 The sentience criterion doesn't ask whether a being resembles humans, but whether it can be harmed or benefited.
  3. P3 Reason, unlike sentience, doesn't determine who can be harmed.
  4. C Therefore, the sentience criterion is not anthropocentric.

The chapter’s central argument in logical form. If the premises are true, the conclusion follows. To disagree, you have to show which premise is false.

They say “You only value sentience because humans are sentient.”

Answer It's the other way around: humans matter because they're sentient, and for the same reason all sentient beings matter.

Original text Luciano Carlos Cunha · Volume VI, p. 14 of the PDF edition · English translation

Proponents of choose a typically human characteristic (for example, a certain way of reasoning) and assign moral standing to nonhuman animals according to their degree of similarity to humans in that characteristic. Defenders of the criterion of are sometimes accused of doing the same9: they notice that sentience is a characteristic of humans, and choose to consider only beings that are sentient (excluding plants, fungi, and bacteria, for example). The objection concludes that, in both cases, only those who have similarities to humans receive consideration (all that changes, in each case, is the characteristic chosen as the standard of similarity).

The main response to this criticism points to two things.

First, there is a difference between using a way of reasoning and using sentience as the criterion for : as we saw in chapter 1, the former does not determine who can be harmed and/or benefited, and the latter does.

Second, it is not that proponents of the criterion of sentience first postulate that humans matter and then look, among the animals they also intend to consider, for some similarity to humans. They are not arguing: “we should consider sentient beings because they resemble humans,” but exactly the opposite: “we should consider humans because they are sentient.” The criterion of sentience would continue to indicate which beings can be harmed and benefited (and, therefore, which should receive moral consideration) even if humans were not sentient. Thus, it is false that the criterion of sentience is, at bottom, anthropocentric.
