Arguments in logical form

The central argument of each chapter, laid out as premises and a conclusion. Study layer: a reconstruction of the author's reasoning, not the original text.

How to read them: if all the premises are true, the conclusion follows. Anyone who rejects the conclusion has to point out which premise is false, and why. That is exactly what the whole collection does with the objections.

Volume I · Discussing Speciesism

An Overview of the Debate on the Moral Consideration of Animals
  1. P1 Discrimination is unfavorable treatment that is unfair.
  2. P2 Giving less weight to harms of the same size just because the victim belongs to another species is unfavorable treatment.
  3. P3 Species alone doesn't explain why someone's harm should count for less.
  4. C Therefore, this treatment is discrimination: speciesism.

Is Species What Matters?
  1. P1 To justify treating someone worse, you have to point to a difference that is relevant to the treatment in question.
  2. P2 Saying “because they're human” or “because they're our species” only repeats the criterion being questioned.
  3. P3 Repeating the criterion instead of justifying it is begging the question.
  4. C Therefore, these appeals don't justify anthropocentrism.

Could Metaphysical Characteristics Justify Speciesism?
  1. P1 What is at stake in moral consideration is whether those affected can be harmed or benefited.
  2. P2 Having a soul or some other metaphysical trait doesn't change whether someone can be harmed or benefited.
  3. P3 Besides, these traits can't be proven.
  4. C Therefore, appealing to them doesn't justify disregarding animals.

Could the Possession of Capacities or Relationships Justify Speciesism?
  1. P1 If the lack of certain abilities in babies and in humans with certain disabilities doesn't justify treating them worse, the lack of those abilities in animals doesn't either.
  2. P2 The lack of these abilities in those humans doesn't justify treating them worse.
  3. P3 Moreover, what matters for consideration is being able to be harmed or benefited, not having these abilities.
  4. C Therefore, having certain abilities or relationships doesn't justify speciesism.

  2. P2 We don't think it's fair to do to these humans what is done to animals.
  3. P3 The reasons not to cause a child suffering exist because suffering is bad, not because of her potential.

Is What Matters Belonging to the Same Species as Those Who Have Certain Capacities?

  2. P2 This demands the abilities from animals but not from humans.
  3. P3 A criterion applied differently to each group is biased.
  4. P4 Moreover, neither species nor abilities are relevant to being able to be harmed.
  5. C Therefore, the appeal to the group doesn't justify speciesism.

  2. P2 They would still be unfair even if race and gender were purely biological categories.
  3. P3 The same goes for species: being a biological category doesn't make it relevant to the weight of suffering.
  4. C Therefore, the distinction between biological and socially constructed doesn't justify speciesism.

  2. P2 In ethics, there are biased positions and positions that aren't, such as the principle of equal consideration.

  2. P2 Describing the origin of an activity is not the same as justifying a position within it.

  4. C Therefore, the appeal to self-interest doesn't justify speciesism.

Attempts to Justify Speciesism That Appeal to Authorities
  1. P1 Either an authority approves of a practice because it is right, or the practice becomes right by being approved.
  2. P2 If the authority approves because it is right, what matters is the reasons, which anyone can evaluate.
  3. P3 If the practice becomes right just by being approved, the approval is arbitrary.
  4. P4 In neither case does the authority, by itself, justify the practice.
  5. C Therefore, the appeal to authority doesn't justify speciesism.

The Appeal to Differentiated Degrees of Moral Status
  1. P1 The Magnitude Thesis says the strength of our reasons depends on the size of the harm or benefit, not on species.
  2. P2 The Status Thesis says some have priority even when they would be harmed less.
  3. P3 A fair rule is one we would choose without knowing which group we'd be in.
  4. P4 Not knowing, we wouldn't choose to give one group a trump card against greater harms suffered by another.
  5. C Therefore, differentiated moral status is unfair.

Attempts to Justify Speciesism That Appeal to Metaethics
  1. P1 Claiming “there's no right or wrong” is a metaethical thesis, not a reason in favor of a normative position.
  2. P2 If it were true, it would apply equally against speciesism and in favor of it.
  3. P3 Those who use it tend to apply it only when the victims are animals, which reveals a double standard.
  4. C Therefore, appeals to metaethics don't justify speciesism.

Volume II · Considering Sentient Beings

What Is Relevant to Knowing to Whom We Owe Consideration?
  1. P1 Moral consideration exists because our decisions can harm or benefit those affected.
  2. P2 So everyone who can be harmed or benefited should be considered.
  3. P3 Defenses of anthropocentrism just assume that their criteria (species, reason, language) are relevant, without explaining why.
  4. C Therefore, the relevant criterion is being able to be harmed or benefited, not these anthropocentric criteria.

What Is the Argument from Species Overlap?
  1. P1 The abilities and relationships used to exclude animals are not present in all humans.
  2. P2 Their absence in babies and in certain humans doesn't justify treating them worse.
  3. P3 If the absence of these traits doesn't justify treating humans worse, it doesn't justify treating animals worse either.
  4. C Therefore, these abilities and relationships don't justify disregarding animals.

What Is Sentience and Why Does It Matter?
  1. P1 To be harmed or benefited, things have to be able to be experienced as good or bad.
  2. P2 Sentience is the capacity to have those experiences.
  3. P3 So only sentient beings can be harmed or benefited.
  4. C Therefore, sentience is what matters for moral consideration.

Which Beings Are Sentient?
  1. P1 We have no direct access to the experiences of other beings, not even of other humans.
  2. P2 We infer sentience from indicators: a centralized nervous system, physiology, overall behavior, and evolutionary advantage.
  3. P3 Vertebrates and many invertebrates, such as octopuses, crustaceans, and insects, show these indicators.
  4. C Therefore, we have good reasons to consider them sentient.

Why Equal Consideration?
  1. P1 Harms and benefits of similar magnitude should be given the same weight.
  2. P2 Giving less weight to a harm just because of species violates this principle.
  3. P3 Speciesism can happen through exploitation, through failing to help, through lesser consideration, or against certain species of animals.
  4. C Therefore, equal consideration requires rejecting all these forms of speciesism.

  2. P2 Nonhuman animals are not accountable agents.

  4. C Therefore, desert doesn't justify failing to prioritize them when they suffer the greater harm.

Is the Rejection of Speciesism Compatible with Only One View of Ethics?
  1. P1 There are very different ethical theories: utilitarianism, maximin, egalitarianism, and others.
  2. P2 They disagree about many situations, but all of them require not being biased when considering the good of those affected.
  3. P3 Speciesism is a form of bias.
  4. C Therefore, rejecting speciesism is compatible with many ethical views, not just with utilitarianism.

If We Reject Speciesism, How Will We Decide Questions of Priority?
  1. P1 In most situations we can't help everyone.
  2. P2 There are priority criteria that don't depend on species: how serious the situation is, the number of victims, the size of the benefit, and future impact.
  3. P3 These criteria are compatible with equal consideration.
  4. C Therefore, it's false that, without species hierarchies, we'd have no guidance for deciding.

  2. P2 In many situations, those who understand less suffer more, because they don't grasp what's happening.
  3. P3 Moreover, lower cognitive capacity usually increases vulnerability.
  4. P4 And even if someone suffered less, that wouldn't justify disregarding them.
  5. C Therefore, a higher degree of cognition doesn't justify the treatment given to animals.

What Would We Decide Under Conditions of Impartiality?
  1. P1 A fair decision is impartial: it's the one we'd choose without knowing which position we'd occupy among those affected.
  2. P2 This test can include not knowing whether we'd be born human or animal.
  3. P3 Not knowing, we wouldn't choose animal exploitation or giving animals less weight.
  4. C Therefore, animal exploitation and speciesism are unfair.

Testing Whether an Attitude Is Speciesist
  1. P1 An attitude is speciesist if it treats sentient beings worse because of their species.
  2. P2 If the decision would change just by changing the species of those affected, or our own position, then species is weighing on the decision.
  3. P3 The practices that affect animals don't pass this test.
  4. C Therefore, besides being unjustified, these practices call on us to do something to change them.

  2. P2 Nonhuman victims are vastly more numerous, in a very serious situation, and much more neglected.
  3. P3 The view that humans should always come first only seems reasonable because of speciesism.
  4. C Therefore, under impartiality, the animal cause should receive very high priority.

Volume III · Animals and the Harm of Death

What Does the Harm of Death Consist Of?
  1. P1 Someone can be harmed by the presence of what's negative or by the absence of what's positive.
  2. P2 Death prevents the individual from enjoying the positive things they would have lived.
  3. P3 So death is a harm, even when it's painless.
  4. C And even if the Epicurean view were correct, it would deny the harm of death to everyone, humans included, not just to animals.

Discussing the Claim That Animals Are Not Harmed by Death
  1. P1 Death harms by preventing the positive things the individual would have lived.
  2. P2 This doesn't depend on the individual understanding what death is or having future-directed desires.
  3. P3 Sentient animals can have a minimally meaningful life ahead of them.
  4. C Therefore, they are harmed by death.

Objections to the Account of the Harm of Death as the Prevention of What Is Positive
  1. P1 The objections point to uncertainty about the future, the asymmetry between the time before birth and after death, and the beings who could exist.
  2. P2 In general, we can estimate whether a life is likely to be positive.
  3. P3 The time of death can be changed; the time of birth typically can't, which explains the asymmetry.
  4. C Therefore, these objections don't overturn the account of the harm of death as deprivation.

Are Those Incapable of Intellectual Pleasures Only Slightly Harmed by Death?
  1. P1 The argument says death harms only slightly those who are incapable of intellectual pleasures.
  2. P2 To show that, it would have to show that non-intellectual pleasures are of little value.
  3. P3 Showing that intellectual pleasures are more valuable doesn't show that.
  4. P4 Moreover, non-intellectual experiences, like being with those we love, can be as valuable as intellectual ones.
  5. C Therefore, animals are not only slightly harmed by death for lacking intellectual pleasures.

How Can We Assess How Much Someone Is Harmed by Death?
  1. P1 Death harms by what it prevents that is positive.
  2. P2 The greater the value someone is deprived of, the greater the harm.
  3. P3 That value depends on the lifetime lost and its quality, measured in QALYs.
  4. C Therefore, the magnitude of the harm of death can be estimated by the QALYs lost, for animals too.

Objections to Assessing the Magnitude of the Harm of Death on the Basis of QALYs
  1. P1 The objection says QALYs have counterintuitive implications, so they should be rejected.
  2. P2 Intuitions can reflect biases, like speciesism, and need to be tested.
  3. P3 The objection offers no alternative criterion grounded independently.
  4. C Therefore, until that happens, we have reasons to rely on QALYs.

Do Animals Have Weaker Psychological Connectedness?
  1. P1 The objection says animals have weak psychological connectedness to the future, and so are only slightly harmed by death.
  2. P2 Simpler minds tend to vary less, which can mean a greater proportion of contents in common.
  3. P3 Moreover, someone who has lived only briefly and would have a good future is greatly harmed by dying, whatever their psychological connectedness.
  4. C Therefore, psychological connectedness doesn't show that animals are only slightly harmed by death.

  1. P1 The thesis says killing an animal is offset by the birth of another with an equally good life.
  2. P2 If that held, it would permit killing children as long as others were born to replace them.
  3. P3 Killing someone who already exists harms someone; not creating a new being harms no one who already exists.
  4. C Therefore, animals are not replaceable.

Are Nonhuman Animals Only Slightly Harmed by Death?
  1. P1 The appeal to psychological connectedness says that prudential reasons (reasons about our own future) depend on the degree of connectedness.
  2. P2 Examples show we have strong reasons to avoid bad events and pursue good ones, even with little psychological connectedness.
  3. P3 So the strength of these reasons doesn't depend on psychological connectedness.
  4. C Therefore, psychological connectedness doesn't show that animals are only slightly harmed by death.

The Claim That Having Little Time Left Makes Death a Small Harm
  1. P1 The claim says those with little time left are only slightly harmed by death.
  2. P2 This is already rejected in the human case: elderly and seriously ill people are still greatly harmed if killed.
  3. P3 Moreover, subjective time may pass differently for smaller animals.
  4. P4 Rejecting this for humans and accepting it for animals would require a special status, which isn't justified.
  5. C Therefore, having little time left doesn't justify killing.

What If Humans Were More Harmed by Death?
  1. P1 The chapter supposes, just for the sake of argument, that humans would be more harmed by death.
  2. P2 It doesn't follow that killing animals is permitted: being less harmed is still being harmed.
  3. P3 Nor does it follow that we may kill some to save others, or that the number of victims doesn't matter.
  4. C Therefore, even with this supposition, the usual conclusions about animals don't follow.

Differentiated Moral Status, the Harm of Death, and the Wrongness of Killing
  1. P1 Direct reasons against killing can depend on the harm of death (the Magnitude Thesis) or on a special status (the Status Thesis).
  2. P2 The two-tier approach gives certain individuals a protected status and treats the rest only by magnitude.
  3. P3 Under impartiality, we wouldn't choose to give this status to one group.
  4. C Therefore, the strength of the reasons against killing should depend only on the magnitude of the harm of death.

  2. P2 There's uncertainty about whether death is annihilation and, if it isn't, whether the afterlife would be better or worse.
  3. P3 On the other hand, we know how the current life would probably go on.
  4. C Therefore, the possibility of life after death doesn't justify killing individuals who have a good life.

Volume IV · The Debate on Animal Exploitation

How Do the Animals Exploited by Humans Live and Die?
  1. P1 Animal exploitation causes intense suffering and death to an enormous number of animals.
  2. P2 Treating humans this way would be considered unacceptable because suffering and dying like that seriously harms the victims.
  3. P3 Exploited animals are also seriously harmed by it.
  4. C Therefore, accepting for them what would be unacceptable for humans is an unjustified double standard.

Why Is Animal Exploitation Unjust?
  1. P1 For it not to violate equal consideration, it would have to be acceptable to do the same to humans facing similar harms.
  2. P2 We wouldn't accept exploiting humans who suffered similar harms.
  3. P3 Under impartiality, we wouldn't accept animal exploitation without knowing whether we'd be human or animal.
  4. C Therefore, animal exploitation is unjust, whatever its production model.

What Is Veganism?
  1. P1 Animals are exploited because there is demand for animal products and services.
  2. P2 Paying for these products causes more animals to be born to suffer and die.
  3. P3 Those who respect animals for their own sake have reasons not to contribute to this.
  4. C Therefore, veganism is the position consistent with respect for animals, and also a way of exposing the injustice they suffer.

What About Plants?

  2. P2 The main indicator of sentience is a centralized nervous system, which plants don't have.
  3. P3 If plants were sentient, raising animals would kill even more plants, since animals eat lots of plants.
  4. C Therefore, the plant objection doesn't justify eating animals; if it held, it would lead to fruitarianism.

The Objection That Consuming Animals Is Natural
  1. P1 “Eating meat is natural” can mean it's possible, instinctive, done by other animals, or part of the food chain.
  2. P2 In none of these senses does being natural imply being justifiable.
  3. P3 Other animals also kill babies and steal, and we don't think that gives us permission to do the same.
  4. C Therefore, the naturalness objection doesn't justify consuming animals.

The Claim That Consuming Animals Puts an End to Their Suffering

  2. P2 In exploitation, it's demand that causes animals to be born into conditions of suffering.
  3. P3 If the concern were real, the right thing would be to stop breeding animals to suffer, not to keep consuming.

Volume V · Differences Between Environmentalism and Consideration for Animals

Respect for animals and environmentalism: complementary or opposed?
  1. P1 Consideration for animals values the good of sentient beings in itself.
  2. P2 Environmentalism, in the sense used here, values non-sentient entities in themselves, such as species and ecosystems.
  3. P3 When these values conflict, environmentalism may prescribe harming animals.
  4. C Therefore, the two positions are not the same thing, nor are they always complementary.

The divergence concerns ends, not only means
  1. P1 Environmentalism and animal advocacy are often seen as having the same goal and differing only in means.
  2. P2 In fact, animal advocacy values sentient beings, and environmentalism values species and ecosystems.
  3. P3 Considering individuals is compatible both with saving the majority and with other positions; considering “the whole” isn't considering the majority.
  4. C Therefore, the disagreement is about ends.

Despite their differences, do animal advocacy and environmentalism converge in practice?
  1. P1 The argument says that since animals need the environment and environmentalism preserves it, the positions converge.
  2. P2 The environment can be preserved in very different configurations.
  3. P3 Environmentalism chooses configurations by criteria like originality, rarity, and biodiversity, not by animals' well-being.
  4. C Therefore, convergence happens in some cases, but not in others.

The divergence does not lie in a focus on the short or the long term
  1. P1 A common belief is that environmentalism thinks long-term and animal advocacy short-term.
  2. P2 Both can consider the long term.
  3. P3 Refusing to help for fear of causing more suffering later is a concern for sentient beings.
  4. C Therefore, the disagreement is about goals, not about time horizon.

Do ecological balance and biodiversity coincide with what is best for animals?
  1. P1 Ecological balance concerns the variation in population size; biodiversity, the variety of species.
  2. P2 Neither notion is based on what is best for sentient beings.

  3. P3 A great deal of benefit is possible with the resources available.
  4. P4 Even if we give less weight to each animal, the number of victims keeps it a priority.
  5. C So the animal cause is a priority by the usual criteria.

Among the Problems Affecting Animals, Which Are the Most Important?
  1. P1 Speciesism leads us to prioritize human problems and, within the animal cause, the animals most similar to us.
  2. P2 Aquatic animals, invertebrates, wild animals, and future beings are victims in astronomically greater numbers.
  3. P3 These problems are highly neglected.
  4. C So there are twofold reasons to focus on them: they are among the most serious, and they are where we can make the most difference.

The Use of Anthropocentric and Environmentalist Arguments in the Animal Cause
  1. P1 Indirect arguments can lead people to cut back their consumption without considering animals.
  2. P2 Different motivations lead to different behavior when circumstances change.
  3. P3 They can reinforce the idea that harm to animals is not enough on its own and strengthen anthropocentric and environmentalist views.
  4. P4 A lack of consideration for sentient beings is one of the main sources of risks of future suffering.

The Total Approach and the Sufficient Approach
  1. P1 Some goals are compatible with a wide range of ethical and political views.
  2. P2 The sufficient approach shows this and broadens acceptance of those goals.
  3. P3 The total approach discusses the complete ideal, including controversial questions.
  4. P4 Using only one of them carries risks: either we never dare to discuss what matters, or we push away people who disagree about everything else.
  5. C So it is important to combine the two approaches.

Shifting the Focus in Debates: Why Is Speciesism Rarely Discussed?
  1. P1 People who defend practices that harm animals tend to keep the focus on things that have nothing to do with the harm to animals.
  2. P2 Animal advocates often accept the shift without noticing.
  3. P3 As long as speciesism is not discussed, people's view of animals does not change.
  4. C So there are no good reasons to accept the shift in focus.

Moral Beliefs, Motivation to Act, and the Role of Ethical Reflection
  1. P1 Some experts write against consuming animals and keep consuming them.
  2. P2 This can happen for several reasons: they do not think it is wrong, think it is only slightly wrong, lack sincerity, have a weak will, or decide not to act.
  3. P3 None of these explanations shows that moral beliefs do not motivate.
  4. P4 And abandoning rational persuasion does not solve anything, since other forms of persuasion have the same limits.
  5. C So ethical reflection still has a central role.

Normative Objections Disguised as Strategic Objections
  1. P1 A normative objection rejects the goal; a strategic one accepts the goal and doubts the means.
  2. P2 Sometimes a normative objection is disguised as a strategic one, so as not to expose the lack of good arguments against the goal.
  3. P3 Whoever claims that a goal is unachievable bears the burden of proving it.
  4. C So there are reasons to reject such claims when they are unsupported.

Focusing on Veganism versus Focusing on Reducing Consumption
  1. P1 We should evaluate strategies by their effects, not by our initial intuitions.
  2. P2 Many advocates of reduction and of veganism share the same goal: the end of exploitation.
  3. P3 Reduction may reach more people; veganism may make the injustice clearer and inspire more engagement.
  4. C So the disagreement is mainly strategic, and it should be treated as such.

Why the Term Welfarism Is Highly Ambiguous
  1. P1 The term welfarism is used for very different positions, in different debates.
  2. P2 There are at least seven meanings, such as accepting "humane" exploitation, advocating reforms, or caring about the well-being of any animal.
  3. P3 Each meaning concerns a distinct debate.
  4. C So accepting or rejecting a position called welfarist does not imply accepting or rejecting the others.

The Debate on Regulating Animal Exploitation
  1. P1 Some people argue that exploitation regulated to reduce suffering is acceptable.
  2. P2 Among those who want abolition, there is debate over whether regulation leads to it and whether it reduces suffering.
  3. P3 Anti-cruelty laws usually require hard-to-obtain proof and exempt industry practices.
  4. C Reforms need to be compared with other strategies, such as cultivated meat, antispeciesist education, and promoting veganism.

The Debate on Cultivated Meat
  1. P1 Cultivated meat is produced from cells, without raising and slaughtering animals.
  2. P2 The objections (it is not natural, the risks, sustainability, the impact on the poor, self-interested producers, it does not solve everything, it still harms animals, it reinforces speciesism) can be answered or are not enough to reject it.
  3. P3 It could enormously reduce exploitation even if people do not change their values.
  4. P4 But focusing only on technology has limits, since values decide how it will be used.

Volume IX · Ethics and the Future

Future Risks for Sentient Beings: An Introduction
  1. P1 New practices and technologies have already multiplied suffering enormously, as factory farming shows.
  2. P2 In the future, the number of sentient beings and the reach of technology could be astronomically greater.
  3. P3 Risks of suffering on that scale (s-risks) carry an enormous expected disvalue, even at a modest probability.
  4. P4 They are easy to ignore and rarely discussed.
  5. C So we need a broad debate about how to prevent them.

Read the chapter →

Temporal Impartiality: What It Is and Why It Matters
  1. P1 We have a bias that makes us give less weight to what happens later.
  2. P2 Suffering is no less bad because it happens in the future.
  3. P3 Impartiality requires not favoring anyone because of irrelevant factors, and timing is one of them.
  4. C So we should give the same weight to future sentient beings, human and nonhuman.

The Objection That Future Beings Are Merely Potential Beings
  1. P1 The objection says we should care only about beings who already exist.
  2. P2 Even if we deny a duty to create new beings, we have duties not to bring beings into existence to suffer and not to harm those who will exist.
  3. P3 The animal cause is already, as a rule, about preventing new animals from being born to suffer.
  4. C So it makes no sense to reject concern for the future on the grounds that it deals with future beings.

Read the chapter →

What Reasons Are There to Focus on the Long Term?
  1. P1 The future may contain far more sentient beings than the present.
  2. P2 It is not guaranteed to be better because of moral progress; that depends on what we do now.
  3. P3 Our decisions can have lasting effects on that future.
  4. C So there are good reasons to adopt a practical focus on the long term.

Read the chapter →

Should We Focus on Preventing S-Risks or X-Risks?
  1. P1 X-risks concern human extinction; s-risks concern astronomical suffering, including that of nonhumans.
  2. P2 Some defenses of focusing on x-risks appeal to speciesist ideas, such as prioritizing humans in order to obtain more resources.
  3. P3 Humanity's survival does not guarantee less suffering and could even expand it.
  4. C So there are reasons to pay more attention to s-risks, without excluding x-risks.

Which Goals Should We Prioritize in the Long Term?
  1. P1 People who want the best impact may disagree about which values to prioritize.
  2. P2 Several views (prioritarian, negative, pluralist) give greater weight to preventing suffering.
  3. P3 You do not need extreme pessimism about the future to adopt this focus.
  4. C So there are reasons to give extra weight to the goals of preventing suffering and premature deaths.

What Does the Focus on S-Risks Consist Of?
  1. P1 A focus on s-risks aims to reduce expected future suffering.
  2. P2 Even if future suffering is "heavy-tailed," it can come from the sum of many risks rather than a few.
  3. P3 So a focus on s-risks does not imply a narrow focus.
  4. C Individually, people can specialize; collectively, we should cover a broad range.

Identifying Risk Factors for S-Risks
  1. P1 It is hard to estimate the long-term consequences of each decision.
  2. P2 But, as in medicine, we can identify factors that increase the likelihood or severity of catastrophes.
  3. P3 Examples: advanced technology, disregard for sentient beings, polarization, malevolent agents with power.
  4. P4 Risks grow when several factors coincide, and prevention efforts reinforce each other too.
  5. C So reducing risk factors is a viable path to preventing s-risks.

Longtermist Strategies: Broad and Targeted
  1. P1 The future is uncertain, with many possible scenarios.
  2. P2 Broad strategies have a positive impact in almost every scenario, but a smaller one in each.
  3. P3 Targeted strategies have a large impact in the scenario they address, but may be useless in others.
  4. P4 Caution is needed: humility, attention to biases, and goals acceptable from many perspectives.
  5. C So we should combine both kinds of strategy, carefully.

Why Focus on Consideration for Sentient Beings and on the Future?
  1. P1 Disregard for sentient beings is one of the main causes of risks of future suffering.
  2. P2 Promoting consideration for all sentient beings tackles that cause.
  3. P3 There are risks, such as using anthropocentric and environmentalist arguments, which can reinforce other views.
  4. P4 We do not yet know the most effective way to prevent s-risks.
  5. C So we should promote consideration for sentient beings and keep an open mind about changing strategies.

Changing Public Debate, Forms of Activism, and Political Institutions
  1. P1 Politics (institutions and public debate) influences the likelihood of s-risks.
  2. P2 Tribalism creates the appearance of deep disagreement and prevents compromise.
  3. P3 Honest debate, broad-range approaches, and democracy reduce risks such as the rise of malevolent agents.
  4. P4 The current situation is so bad that even small improvements would make a big difference.
  5. C So improving politics is crucial for avoiding s-risks.

Shaping Emerging Technologies to Prevent Future Suffering
  1. P1 New technologies could give enormous power to people who do not care about those affected.
  2. P2 AI, space colonization, and the creation of new forms of sentience could generate astronomical suffering.
  3. P3 Shaping these technologies early, with non-anthropocentric values, reduces these risks.
  4. C Spreading consideration for all sentient beings as widely as possible helps both to avoid these technologies and to reduce the harm if they are created.

The Influence Triad and Future Suffering
  1. P1 New technologies could give great power to agents who do not care about those affected.
  2. P2 An agent's results depend on the combination of intention, knowledge, and power.
  3. P3 Power without knowledge risks causing harm even with good intentions; knowledge without power is ineffective.
  4. C So to prevent future suffering, we need to increase the knowledge and power of those who want to benefit sentient beings, and limit those of others.

Biases That Lead Us to Neglect the Problems with the Most Victims
  1. P1 Biases are tendencies that systematically lead to faulty reasoning, and they are different from fallacies.
  2. P2 Several biases make our compassion shrink as the number of victims grows.
  3. P3 So the problems with the most victims tend to be the most neglected.
  4. C To correct this, we should learn about these biases and focus on people who want to reason honestly.

Biases That Incline Us to Neglect the Situation of Wild Animals
  1. P1 The situation of wild animals involves one of the largest numbers of victims.
  2. P2 Biases such as status quo bias, the just-world hypothesis, and agent detection incline us to see it as good or justified.
  3. P3 Other biases make us doubt that helping is feasible and underestimate how receptive the public is.
  4. C So this neglect is partly an effect of biases that we can learn to correct.

Biases That Contribute to the Neglect of the Long Term and of S-Risks
  1. P1 Biases incline us to give less importance to questions about the future.
  2. P2 Others make us ignore the expected value of scenarios that are unlikely but very serious.
  3. P3 Overconfidence in our intuitions reinforces these errors.
  4. C So to take s-risks seriously, we need an open mind and a willingness to change our position in the face of good reasons.

Cognitive Biases and Their Influence on Our Decisions That Affect Animals
  1. P1 Revising our beliefs about facts, ethics, and strategies requires overcoming several psychological tendencies.
  2. P2 Biases incline us to defend what already exists, to trust ourselves too much, and to accept bad arguments when they favor us.
  3. P3 They affect both people who consume animals and people who defend them.
  4. C So knowing about them is essential for discussing and deciding better.

  3. P3 Sometimes the people who raise them do not even believe them, but use them as excuses.

Volume XI · How Do the Various Ethical Theories Approach the Animal Question?

An Introduction to the Central Distinctions in Value Theory
  1. P1 Questions about what is good or bad are different from questions about what we ought to do.
  2. P2 Something can have value in itself or only as a means to something else.
  3. P3 A situation can be good in one respect and bad in another.
  4. C So judging how good a situation is all things considered requires distinguishing and weighing these respects.

Read the chapter →

An Introduction to Theories of Well-Being
  1. P1 There are monist theories of well-being (a single aspect) and pluralist ones (several aspects).
  2. P2 They disagree in many cases, but they converge to a minimal degree.
  3. P3 The typical situations of exploited animals and wild animals are bad according to any of these theories.
  4. C So whichever theory of well-being we adopt, animals are seriously harmed in these situations.

Assessing How Good/Bad a Situation Containing Several Individuals Is
  1. P1 A situation involving many individuals can be evaluated in several respects: the total, priority, equality, sufficiency, and desert.
  2. P2 Monist theories consider a single aspect; pluralist theories consider several.
  3. P3 These aspects can be valued in themselves or only as means.
  4. C When they conflict, we have to decide how to weigh them.

Read the chapter →

Value Judgments, Descriptions of Facts, Objectivity, and Subjectivity
  1. P1 "Subjective" can mean "related to consciousness" or "without objective truth."
  2. P2 Everyone who suffers experiences suffering as negative in itself.
  3. P3 Some things cannot be explained without appealing to their value or disvalue.
  4. C So judgments like "suffering is bad" can have a high degree of certainty, even if there is disagreement about what to do.

Categories of Normative Ethical Theories
  1. P1 Consequentialism focuses on consequences; deontology, on rules; character ethics, on character traits.
  2. P2 They differ at the foundational level (why something matters), but often agree at the factoral level (what to do).
  3. P3 The arguments for equal consideration of sentient beings can be accepted from within any of these schools.
  4. C So the major theories tend to converge on equal consideration.

To What Extent Do Ethical Theories Agree About What We Owe Animals?
  1. P1 Goal-based theories (utilitarianism, prioritarianism, egalitarianism, sufficientarianism, maximin) do not give different weight to equal well-being because of species.
  2. P2 Theories that are not goal-based (Kantianism, discourse ethics, contractualism, virtue ethics, ethics of care), when applied consistently, also include sentient beings.
  3. P3 Desert and pluralist possibilities do not change this conclusion.
  4. C So the main theories imply equal consideration, the rejection of exploitation, and reasons to help wild animals.

Weighing Criteria
  1. P1 Situations can be good in some respects and bad in others.
  2. P2 There are many ways to weigh these respects, such as trumps, fixed weights, diminishing returns, and sufficiency.
  3. P3 Each way has advantages and problems, such as arbitrariness or difficulty of calculation.
  4. C So weighing criteria is a difficult task that requires making the chosen method and its reasons explicit.

Attempts to Justify Disregarding Animals
  1. P1 The default position says that the use of animals is fully justified.
  2. P2 Each justification appeals to a criterion: species, capacities, nature, tradition, authority, and so on.
  3. P3 None explains why that criterion would be relevant to whether someone can be harmed.
  4. C So the default position is not justified.

The Case for Considering All Sentient Beings
  1. P1 If lacking certain capacities does not justify treating humans worse, it does not justify treating animals worse either (species overlap).
  2. P2 What is relevant to consideration is being able to be harmed and benefited, which depends on sentience (relevance).
  3. P3 Equal harms and benefits should carry the same weight (equal consideration).
  4. P4 If we did not know whether we would be born human or animal, we would not accept the current treatment (impartiality).
  5. C So every sentient being should receive full moral consideration.

The Debate on the Sentience Criterion
  1. P1 The sentience criterion tells us which beings should be considered, not which ethical theory to adopt.
  2. P2 It is not anthropocentric, it is not animal-kingdomism, and it does not reduce harm to suffering.
  3. P3 It includes many invertebrates and does not imply that values are subjective.
  4. P4 The criterion of life does not show that nonsentient beings can be harmed.
  5. C So the criticisms of the sentience criterion have solid answers.

The Harm of Death and the Reasons Against Killing
  1. P1 Death deprives someone of the positive things they would have lived, whether or not they understand what death is.
  2. P2 This does not depend on desires about the future, intellectual pleasures, or psychological connectedness.
  3. P3 Replacing one animal with another does not make up for the harm to the one who died.
  4. C So killing animals, even painlessly, harms them.

Differences Between Environmentalism and Consideration for Animals
  1. P1 The anthropocentric position wants the environment to be better for humans; the sentience-centered one, better for sentient beings; the environmentalist one, kept in certain configurations even if that is worse for them.
  2. P2 Environmentalist programs do not aim to save sentient beings, whether they are the majority or the minority.
  3. P3 Balance and biodiversity do not measure the well-being of animals.
  4. C So environmentalism and animal advocacy differ in their ends, not just in their means.

The Situation of Wild Animals and the Proposal to Help Them
  1. P1 Our reasons to prevent harm depend on its magnitude, not on whether its origin is human or natural.
  2. P2 Animals in the wild suffer enormous harms, and most are born only to die young.
  3. P3 Even discounting destroyed eggs, the number of victims is gigantic.
  4. C So the situation of wild animals is as important as it would be if the harms were caused by humans.

  1. P1 The objections claim that helping would be arrogant, anthropocentric, paternalistic, or contrary to freedom and ecology.
  2. P2 Many are self-contradictory or apply to animals what they would never apply to humans.
  3. P3 Helping is what allows animals to live and exercise their freedom.
  4. C So these objections do not justify failing to help.

The Debate on the Proposal to Help Wild Animals (Part 2)
  1. P1 The objections say that we are responsible only for human practices, or that the proposal is too demanding or impractical.

Changing Public Debate and Political Institutions
  1. P1 Dysfunctional politics encourages polarization and increases the risk of malevolent agents in power.
  2. P2 Thinking in nuances, evaluating arguments rather than people, and being intellectually honest reduce tribalism.
  3. P3 Democracy protects against the concentration of power and guarantees the freedom to raise ethical concerns.
  4. C So improving debate and institutions, even a little, greatly reduces the risks of future suffering.
