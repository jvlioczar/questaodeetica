Before you read study layer, not part of the original text

Not being able to help everyone is no reason to help no one, and what matters is the actual amount of benefit, not the percentage of the problem solved; that is why focusing on wild animals can be very effective.

We cannot save every animal in nature. But every animal who is helped feels the difference. And helping a "small part" of a gigantic problem can help more animals than solving a small problem completely.

Solving 0.1% of a problem with a quadrillion victims means helping a trillion, which is more than solving 90% of a problem with a trillion victims.

  1. P1 The objection says that, since we cannot help everyone, we should help no one, or that we would only help a small fraction of the problem.
  2. P2 For each animal helped, the help makes all the difference.
  3. P3 What matters is the actual amount of benefit, not the percentage of the problem.
  4. C So focusing on the suffering of wild animals can be a very effective way to reduce suffering in the world.

The chapter’s central argument in logical form. If the premises are true, the conclusion follows. To disagree, you have to show which premise is false.

They say “There are so many that we will never manage it. So there is no point.”

Answer We cannot manage to help every human in need either, and that has never been a reason to help none. Every individual helped counts.

Original text Luciano Carlos Cunha · Volume VII, p. 72 of the PDF edition · English translation

25.1. The objection that it is not possible to help everyone in need

The number of animals in the wild is gigantic. According to some estimates, there are somewhere between 1 and 10 quintillion individuals in the wild at any given moment79. To get an idea of this number, if we make an analogy with the span of one year and compare it with the human population, the latter would represent at most 0.25 seconds of the year.

It is sometimes argued that it is pointless to help animals in the wild because, given the astronomical number of victims, we will never manage to help them all. However, this objection can easily be answered by pointing out that the impossibility of helping everyone neither prevents nor renders pointless helping in those cases where we can do so. For the animals we could help, our help would make all the difference.

Objections of this kind would never be taken seriously in the human context. Imagine, for example, a doctor refusing to save your life on the grounds that doing so would be pointless because it is impossible to help all the humans who need it. It is only because of the prevalence of speciesism that objections of this kind arise when nonhuman animals are the victims.

25.2. Should we prioritize problems of which we would solve a larger share?

There is another objection to the proposal to help which says that, even if we manage to help in an enormous number of cases, doing so would not be an efficient way of using our resources since, given the astronomical number of victims, even if we helped many of them, this would still represent a small fraction of the total. According to this objection, we should focus only on exploited animals, since the percentage of the problem we would be able to solve would be greater.

The main problem with this objection is that it makes the mistake of measuring efficiency in the use of resources by percentage, rather than by the actual amount of benefit we could produce. Let us look at an example to illustrate this point.

Let us imagine that with the same amount of resources we could either help 90% of the animals in problem A, or help, to the same extent, 0.1% of the animals in problem B (who are all in a situation as bad as that of the animals in problem A).

If we are guided by proportion, we will choose to help 90% of the animals in problem A. But suppose that problem A involves one trillion animals, and problem B involves one quadrillion. 90% of one trillion is 900 billion. 0.1% of one quadrillion is one trillion. If we choose to solve 0.1% of problem B, we will be helping 100 billion more animals with the same amount of resources.

This reveals how mistaken it is to rely on percentage to measure efficiency in the use of resources. In the example above, if we are guided by percentage, we will fail to help 100 billion animals we could have helped. Therefore, to measure efficiency in the use of the resources we have for helping, what matters is the actual amount of benefit we could produce, not how many similar cases remain to be solved.

Thus, the fact that, even if we helped animals in the wild on a large scale, this could still represent a small percentage of the total number of victims of the problem does not show that doing so would not be an efficient way of using the resources available to us to reduce suffering. What would have to be assessed, among other things, is: (1) how large a benefit we could produce with the same amount of resources by investing in this or that problem; and (2) how each problem is (since this reveals in which problems other people are likely to invest their resources anyway).

Taking these two factors into account, we can see that focusing on preventing wild animal suffering can be a very efficient way of preventing suffering in the world. For example, regarding the first factor, reducing the number of births in animal species that maximize the number of offspring would drastically reduce the number of individuals who would be born to lives full of suffering and a premature death. As we saw in Chapter 3, the reproductive potential of these animals is so gigantic that it exceeds the numbers of by several orders of magnitude. For example, sterilizing a single ocean sunfish would prevent at least 300 million animals from being born to this fate (and more than that if the sunfish in question would reproduce more than once in its lifetime). Carrying out an aid program of this kind does not seem to be more difficult than, for example, managing to shut down a factory farm (in fact, it may be much easier). As for the second factor, the issue of wild animal suffering is highly neglected, even by animal advocates.

All this shows that, far from it being pointless to focus on the issue of wild animal suffering, such a focus has great potential to be an extremely efficient way of preventing or minimizing suffering in the world.

How the author sums up this debate in The Essentials of Animal Ethics.

There is no way to help them all. Hence, we should not help any. (VII, ch. 25).
Yes, it is true that there is no way to help them all. But for each animal helped, the help makes all the difference. There is also no way to help all domesticated animals, or all humans, but that does not imply that we should not help them. Something like this is said only in the case of helping nonhuman animals because of the prevalence of speciesism. None of us would refuse to be helped just because it is not possible to help everyone in need. Therefore, such a position violates impartiality.
We should first solve the problems that humans cause animals. If we cannot even solve the problems that humans cause animals, how much less can we solve the harms caused by natural processes. (VII, ch. 25).
If our goal is to help as efficiently as possible, we should not base ourselves on the origin of the harm (whether or not it is caused by humans), but on the amount of benefit that could be produced with each strategy. It is not necessary to solve all (or even any) of the problems caused by humans in order to do something for animals who are victims of natural processes. Helping animals who are victims of natural processes is not necessarily harder than helping animals who are victims of human practices. In fact, in some cases it may be easier, since there may be less resistance. What we should do is investigate where our resources, if applied, would produce the greater good (for example, would help more victims, or would help those in the worst situation, etc.), and not investigate whether the harm originated in human practices or in natural processes.
The problem has so many victims that, even if we managed to help on a large scale, it would still be a small percentage of the problem (VII, ch. 25).
The percentage does not matter; what matters is the actual amount of benefit we could produce. For example, suppose that with the same resource we can either help 90% of the animals in problem A or help to the same extent 0.1% of the animals in problem B (who are all in a situation as bad as that of the animals in problem A). If we are guided by the percentage, we will help the 90%. But suppose problem A involves a trillion animals and problem B a quadrillion. The point is: 90% of a trillion is 900 billion, whereas 0.1% of a quadrillion is a trillion. Solving 0.1% of problem B means helping 100 billion more animals with the same resource.
