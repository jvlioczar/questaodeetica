Before you read study layer, not part of the original text

The accusation of "playing God" does not hold up: we accept many interventions in nature to benefit humans or achieve environmentalist goals; those who make it are not against intervening, but against benefiting animals.

We build houses, make vaccines, and grow food, and all of that is changing nature. Nobody calls that arrogance. So helping a sick animal should not be called that either.

Someone who calls vaccinating animals "arrogant" while applauding the construction of a dam is applying a double standard.

  1. P1 The accusation says that helping wild animals is arrogant because it intervenes in nature.
  2. P2 Almost everyone accepts interventions in nature to benefit humans or for environmentalist goals.
  3. P3 So intervening in nature is not, in itself, the problem.
  4. C The accusation reveals opposition to the goal of benefiting animals, not to intervention.

The chapter’s central argument in logical form. If the premises are true, the conclusion follows. To disagree, you have to show which premise is false.

They say “Who are we to interfere with nature?”

Answer We are the same people who interfere all the time, with medicine, agriculture, and cities. And not helping also decides the fate of animals.

Original text Luciano Carlos Cunha · Volume VII, p. 21 of the PDF edition · English translation

The proposal to help is sometimes accused of being arrogant because it involves interventions in the natural course of things19. It is commonly said that those who advocate helping wild animals are “playing God.” Does this accusation make sense?

Interventions in the natural course of things are widely accepted and practiced, as long as the goal is or environmentalist. For example, building homes, schools, hospitals, and libraries, agriculture, medicine, and interventions to keep ecosystems in the configurations valued by are not accused of arrogance or of “playing God.” Yet they involve massive interventions in the natural course of things.

Indeed, helping animals who are victims of is widely accepted and practiced, as long as the goal is anthropocentric or environmentalist. One example is vaccinating wild animals in order to prevent the transmission of disease to humans, to animals who live alongside humans, or to animals whom humans intend to exploit directly. Another example is vaccinating wild animals to preserve endangered species.

This suggests that, despite what they say, those who make the accusation of arrogance are not opposed to intervening in nature or in natural processes. Rather, they are opposed to the goal of benefiting nonhuman animals.

Moreover, there is nothing in this objection that actually shows that it is wrong to intervene in the natural course of things. So even if there were someone who rejected any and every intervention in nature (whether to benefit nonhuman animals, to benefit humans, or to achieve environmentalist goals), this would not show that their position is correct.

At other times it is argued that the arrogance lies not in intervening in natural processes, but in the fact that, by choosing to help, we are deciding the fate of those animals. Obviously, however, by deciding not to help we are equally deciding their fate. The difference is that, if we choose not to help, the fate of these animals will be suffering and death.

A similar objection arises when it is said that those who advocate helping animals are arrogant because they are trying to make the world the way they want it to be. But, again, when someone takes a stand against helping, they too are trying to make the world the way they want it to be. In one case, the person wants the world to stay as it is, and in the other, the person wants to change it. But in both cases people are equally trying to make the world the way they want.

This chapter in Volume XII

How the author sums up this debate in The Essentials of Animal Ethics.

Intervening in nature (that is, in natural territory and in natural processes) is always wrong, since it is an arrogant attitude. It amounts to “playing God.” (VII, ch. 6).
There are many interventions in nature that are accepted by almost everyone. For example, those made to benefit humans (medicine, agriculture, the construction of housing, schools, hospitals, libraries, etc.) and to achieve environmentalist goals (interventions to preserve rare species, to keep ecosystems in a certain configuration, etc.). If any of these interventions is justifiable, then it makes no sense to say that it is wrong to help wild animals just because it would involve intervening in nature.
It is wrong to influence the fate of these animals. (VII, ch. 6).
We will be influencing the fate of these animals either way, whether we decide to help or decide not to help.
