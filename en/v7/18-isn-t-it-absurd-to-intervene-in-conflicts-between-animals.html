Isn't It Absurd to Intervene in Conflicts Between Animals?

Before you read study layer, not part of the original text

Intervening in conflicts between animals is not absurd: we already intervene in predation on a large scale to protect humans, exploited animals, and environmental goals; the question is whether and how to do it for the good of the animals, without making things worse.

People already stop wolves from attacking sheep on farms, for example. So getting involved in these conflicts is not impossible. The question is whether, with care and study, we could also do it to help the animals themselves.

If we already use fences to protect cattle from predators, it is not absurd to think about ways to protect other animals, as long as it does not cause more harm.

  1. P1 Large-scale interventions in predation have been practiced for decades to protect humans, exploited animals, or environmental goals.
  2. P2 So intervening in conflicts between animals is not absurd in itself.
  3. P3 If an intervention has a good chance of making things worse, the proposal itself advises against it.
  4. C As knowledge grows, more safe interventions may become possible.

The chapter’s central argument in logical form. If the premises are true, the conclusion follows. To disagree, you have to show which premise is false.

They say “Wanting to stop the lion from hunting is absurd.”

Answer We already stop predators from hunting when the victim is human or livestock. The proposal is not to start intervening right now, but to study what would help without making things worse.

Original text Luciano Carlos Cunha · Volume VII, p. 45 of the PDF edition · English translation

In the wild, animals routinely engage in conflicts with animals of other species33 (as in cases of and parasitism) and with animals of the same species34 (as in disputes over food and territory, and also in sexual conflicts35). It is sometimes claimed that the proposal to help is absurd because it implies intervening in such conflicts36. In this chapter we will discuss the general question of conflicts between animals. The question of parasitism will be discussed in more detail in Chapter 30, and the question of predation in Chapter 31.

There are two variations of this objection. The first says that intervening in these conflicts is wrong because it could result in something even worse than doing nothing. For example, it is commonly pointed out that if we prevent cases of predation, prey animals could reproduce even more, and the result could be even more suffering and more deaths than if nothing were done (for example, through an increase in the number of deaths from starvation due to the increase in the number of births).

One problem with this first variation is that what the proposal to help advocates is researching ways to make the outcome over time better than if nothing were done. Therefore, such a proposal would not say to intervene in cases where doing so would have even worse results than doing nothing. Nor would it say not to plan interventions. Poorly planned interventions could certainly have even worse results than doing nothing. But this does not show that well-planned interventions would have worse results than doing nothing. For example, interventions that directly or indirectly reduced (that is, the plants that form the base of the food chain) would tend to reduce the total number of births37 and would therefore escape the objection that the intervention would risk increasing the number of births38.

The second variation of the objection, by contrast, holds that even if doing nothing results in more suffering and deaths over time, the right thing is not to intervene. For example, it is sometimes said that the fact that predators and parasites need to attack other organisms in order to survive makes it wrong to intervene.

We will reject this variation if we reject the idea that an action can be right while having even worse results than other available options. Moreover, it could be pointed out that this variation is , since when the victims are human, predators and parasites also need to attack in order to survive, yet it is not said that this makes it wrong to rescue the victims.

Large-scale interventions in predation have been widely practiced for many decades39. These interventions aim to protect humans or domesticated animals whom humans intend to exploit. At other times, they aim to achieve goals, such as preserving rare species of animals or plants. This suggests that a double standard is commonly adopted: if the intervention has an or environmentalist goal, it is accepted without having to prove that it will have better results; if the intervention is centered on concern for the good of animals, it is rejected even if it has better results. This is the result of the predominance of speciesism.

It is sometimes argued that conflicts between animals do not raise an ethical problem because the animals themselves cannot be held responsible40. But we already saw in Chapter 2 the problems with this kind of view: just as when humans are victims of , we have reasons to try to help the victims because suffering is negative, and it is negative regardless of whether or not it originated from a responsible agent41.

At other times it is argued that intervening would be taking sides and would therefore violate impartiality. However, this argument confuses neutrality with . What impartiality requires is not favoring or disfavoring anyone in a biased way. For example, impartiality implies that levels of harm and benefit of similar magnitude in different individuals receive the same weight. This does not imply that, if we do this,

we will conclude that we should not intervene in the situation. For example, if someone has taken into account all the affected animals, whether predators or prey, given equal weight to the good of each, and concluded that the right thing is to intervene because in this way there will be less suffering and fewer premature deaths over time, they are not being biased. On the contrary: they are doing exactly what they would do if they did not know who those affected were (thus meeting the requirement of impartiality). On the other hand, holding that it is right to intervene only if the victims are human is speciesist and therefore violates impartiality.

None of this necessarily means that, given the current state of knowledge, intervening in such conflicts would be the best thing to do to prevent the harms animals suffer in the wild (although it may mean that, as more knowledge about this issue becomes available, preventing such conflicts from occurring might become a promising way of reducing natural harms). It does show, however, that intervening in such conflicts is not wrong in itself.

This chapter in Volume XII

How the author sums up this debate in The Essentials of Animal Ethics.

Intervening in conflicts between animals could make the situation even worse (for example, intervening in predation could cause a population explosion of prey). (VII, ch. 18, 31).
In any case where intervening has a good probability of making the situation even worse because of knock-on consequences, the proposal to help wild animals would prescribe not intervening. However, this does not mean that intervening will always have worse consequences. For example, by reducing primary productivity, the tendency is for populations in general to decrease, since it is a “bottom-up” kind of intervention in the food chain.
The proposal would entail intervening in conflicts between animals, and this is wrong even when doing so improves the situation. (VII, ch. 18, 31).
This objection assumes that the right action sometimes has worse consequences than other available courses of action. However, this thesis would have to be demonstrated, and the objection already assumes it to be correct in advance. Moreover, when humans are the victims of animals, this thesis is not defended. When humans are the victims, it is held that it is always right to help, regardless of the consequences. When animals are the victims, it is held that it is always wrong to help, even if the consequences of not helping are worse. This is speciesism.
There is nothing that can be done on a large scale (VII, ch. 18, 24, 31, 36, 37).
Many things could be done on a large scale. For example, it could be studied which elements in the environment tend to reduce primary productivity (and, consequently, total suffering). In addition, the human practices that contribute to natural processes maximizing the number of births (and, consequently, suffering) could be avoided. This alone would enormously reduce the number of animals who would be born only to suffer.
