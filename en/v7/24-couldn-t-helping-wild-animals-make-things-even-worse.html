Before you read study layer, not part of the original text

The fear of making things worse and the appeal to ecological balance do not justify not helping: if we cannot predict the long term, we also do not know that not helping is better; the answer is more research, and balance can coexist with enormous suffering.

"What if helping makes everything worse?" That is an important question. But "what if not helping is worse?" is too. The answer is not to sit back and do nothing, but to study more so we can help safely.

A doctor who does not know whether a treatment is safe does not conclude that not treating is safe: the doctor does research.

  1. P1 The objection says that helping may make things worse in the long run, or throw ecosystems out of balance.
  2. P2 If we cannot predict the long term, we also do not know that not helping has better consequences.
  3. P3 Ecological balance depends on arbitrary reference points and can coexist with enormous suffering.
  4. C So the solution is to thoroughly research the factors that affect animals' well-being, not to let nature take its course.

The chapter’s central argument in logical form. If the premises are true, the conclusion follows. To disagree, you have to show which premise is false.

They say “Nature balances itself; better not to interfere.”

Answer Balance, here, means that almost all the young die and the population stays stable. It is stable, but it is not good for the animals.

24.1. Concern about long-term consequences

One of the main objections to the proposal to prevent/minimize the harms that animals in the wild suffer as a result of is the concern that, in trying to help, we might inadvertently make the situation even worse71. This concern arises from the observation that ecosystems involve a series of very complex interactions, which makes it difficult to predict what the long-term consequences would be72.

Although this objection involves an important concern, we need to take into account three points suggesting that there is a greater likelihood of achieving better results if this issue is researched in depth than if it is set aside and nature is left to take its course. These points will be explained below.

The first is that it makes no sense to claim, first, that we do not know how long-term consequences will unfold, and then that we know that letting nature take its course has better long-term consequences than trying to help.

The second is that there are various interventions that have been carried out for decades and have benefited many animals, without the negative effects the objection fears73. This is the case with large-scale vaccination of , which has been carried out for decades for or reasons but benefits the vaccinated animals. The knowledge gained from these and similar programs could also be used with the goal of benefiting the animals themselves, and more knowledge could be obtained if there were research focused on the good of the animals themselves.

The third is that it seems clear that the more is invested in studying this issue in depth, the greater the likelihood of knowing how long-term consequences are likely to unfold and, therefore, of achieving the best results. This is, therefore, a reason to reject the idea that "letting nature take its course" would achieve the best

results. For example, we also do not know all the possible long-term repercussions of helping humans. However, no one says that this shows that we should not help these humans and that it is better to let nature take its course.

The objection may be assuming that, if nature is left to take its course, the outcome is predominantly positive for animals. But, as we saw in Chapter 3, this is false. Alternatively, it may be assuming that we are responsible to a greater degree for the consequences of helping than for the consequences of not helping. However, as we saw in Chapter 21, this is arbitrary since, in both cases, the outcomes will turn out one way or another depending on our choice.

24.2. The appeal to ecological balance

It could be objected that there is a reason to think that, if nature is left to take its course, the outcome will probably be better for animals: "nature balances itself." According to this objection, the result of letting nature take its course is , and this coincides with what is best for animals. Therefore, according to this objection, if we want what is best for animals, we need only let nature take its course. Below, we will look at the three main problems with this objection.

The first is that whether a situation is considered balanced or not depends on factors that are, to some extent, arbitrary. For example, it will depend on how different the situation is from a previous state taken as a reference point. However, any previous state can be chosen as a reference point and, depending on which state is chosen and how often the measurement is taken, one and the same state may be considered balanced or unbalanced74.

The second is that, in nature, there is no balance of the kind common sense imagines. In nature, populations fluctuate enormously, almost always irregularly75. In fact, entire ecosystems and species arise and go extinct as a result of natural processes, also generally irregularly and at irregular intervals.

It could be objected that this is precisely what is meant by balance: whatever results from natural processes, no matter what it is. But if so, then what is being done is arbitrarily changing the definition of balance in order to make the claim that nature is balanced true. That is, faced with the finding that a claim is false, one changes the definition of the term to make it true. With this maneuver, it is obvious that everything that results from natural processes will be balanced, since the definition of "balanced" has been changed to "everything that results from natural processes." But if that is the definition, then saying "everything that results from natural processes is balanced" is redundant, since it would amount to saying "everything that results from natural processes results from natural processes."

The third, and main, problem is that a situation can be balanced in the sense of being stable and yet be terribly bad for the animals affected. For example, situations like those we saw in Chapter 3, where over generations on average only two young survive per brood (something common in nature), are balanced in this sense, but, given the gigantic size of the broods, they are also a scenario in which suffering and the number of premature deaths are maximized.

It could be objected that, if there were less balance, then the amount of suffering and premature death would be even greater. However, there is not necessarily such a correlation. Everything will depend on how a greater or lesser degree of balance influences other factors that directly affect the good of beings. For example, it will depend on whether it results in a greater number of births in species most of whose members have lives full of suffering or in species whose members have better chances of having positive lives. It will also depend on how it affects factors such as the availability of food and shelter and the amount of disease and conflict among animals. Moreover, since ecosystems tend to reach equilibrium at high levels of plant and animal productivity, disturbances probably tend to reduce, rather than increase, suffering76.

That is, there is no direct correlation between higher levels of ecological balance and a better overall balance for sentient beings. Therefore, if the goal is to achieve the best

state of affairs for animals, we should directly investigate how they are positively or negatively affected, rather than centering the analysis on the degree of balance77.

A few years ago, the creation of a field of research was proposed, welfare biology, which would study how animals are affected in their environments from the standpoint of what is better or worse for them as sentient individuals who have well-being78. This field could inform projects to help animals in the wild. As knowledge in increased, the level of safety and efficiency of aid projects would increase.

Therefore, given what we have seen above, the solution to the problem of wild animal suffering is not to let nature take its course, but rather to research ever more deeply the factors that affect the quality of life of animals in their ecosystems, so that ever safer and more efficient aid projects become possible, just as is already done when the victims are human.

How the author sums up this debate in The Essentials of Animal Ethics.

There is nothing that can be done on a large scale (VII, ch. 18, 24, 31, 36, 37).
Many things could be done on a large scale. For example, it could be studied which elements in the environment tend to reduce primary productivity (and, consequently, total suffering). In addition, the human practices that contribute to natural processes maximizing the number of births (and, consequently, suffering) could be avoided. This alone would enormously reduce the number of animals who would be born only to suffer.
It is hard to predict long-term consequences. Helping could make things even worse. Better not to try to help. (VII, ch. 24).
If it is hard to predict long-term consequences, then we cannot know for certain that not helping has the best consequences. What we would have to do is research ever more thoroughly how interactions in ecosystems take place (among animals and between them and their surroundings). By doing so, our knowledge of the probability of long-term knock-on consequences would keep increasing, which would make it possible to plan ever safer and more efficient programs of help.
Nature already balances itself, and this coincides with what is best for animals. Better not to intervene. (VII, ch. 24).
In fact, populations naturally fluctuate enormously, almost always irregularly. Moreover, a situation can be stable and yet terribly bad for the animals affected. For example, situations in which, over generations, an average of only two offspring per brood survive are balanced in this sense, but, given the enormous size of the broods, it is also a scenario in which suffering and premature deaths are maximized.
But if there were less balance, there would be even more suffering (VII, ch. 24).
Not necessarily. Everything depends on how the degree of balance or imbalance would influence other factors that directly affect animals (such as the number of births and in which species, the availability of food and shelter, the amount of disease and conflict, etc.). Thus, if the goal is what is best for animals, we should investigate these factors directly, and not the degree of balance. Moreover, since ecosystems tend to settle into balance at high levels of plant and animal productivity, disturbances probably tend to reduce, rather than increase, suffering.
