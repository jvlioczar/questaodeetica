Volume VII · Advanced level

Ethics and the Situation of Wild Animals

An analysis of the question of natural harms

37 chaptersabout 3 hours of reading179 notes179 references

0 of 37 read

About this volume

This book describes how natural processes typically affect animals living in the wild, presents the foundations of the proposal to help these animals, and also covers the debate surrounding this proposal.

ISBN 978-65-01-49140-0 · 1st edition, May 2025. Original text by Luciano Carlos Cunha, translated into English.

In a nutshell

The vast majority of animals live in the wild, and most of them are born only to die young from hunger, cold, disease or predation. This volume argues that we have reasons to help them and answers more than thirty objections, from "arrogance" to parasitism, predation and the destruction of nature.

For kids

In nature, most babies die soon after they are born, hungry or sick. If we care about animals, we cannot care only about the ones humans hurt: we also want to help the ones who suffer because of nature, carefully and with science.

You will learn

  • How natural processes affect wild animals
  • The central argument for helping them
  • Replies to the most common objections (arrogance, freedom, paternalism, ecology)
  • What is already being done and what welfare biology could do

It helps to have read: Volumes I, II and V.

Chapters

  1. 1
    What Should We Do About Animals Living in the Wild?

    If we really care about animals, we will not limit our concern to harms caused by humans: for the animal, it makes no difference whether the harm comes from a human or from hunger, disease, and natural disasters.

    2 min
  2. 2
    How Not to Understand the Issue

    Saying that natural harms are not a matter of ethics because no one can hold nature responsible is a mistake: the question is about what we ought to do; even saying that helping is optional is already an ethical answer.

    2 min
  3. 3
    How Do Natural Processes Typically Affect Wild Animals?

    In nature, hunger, disease, parasites, cold, and predation are the rule; most species have enormous numbers of offspring, almost all of whom die soon after birth. The number of victims makes even exploitation look small.

    6 min
  4. 4
    The View That It Is Not a Problem Because the Victims Are Nonhuman Animals

    The idea that the suffering of wild animals matters only if it affects humans or environmentalist goals is speciesist: whatever explains why the good of humans matters in itself implies that the good of any sentient being also matters, and to the same degree.

    4 min
  5. 5
    Foundations of the Proposal to Help Victims of Natural Processes

    Three arguments ground helping: the argument from the existence of reasons (harm is bad whatever its origin), the argument from the strength of reasons (which depends on magnitude), and the argument from the weight of the criterion (even giving more weight to human-caused harms, the scale of natural harms would still give strong reasons).

    3 min
  6. 6
    Is the Proposal to Help Wild Animals Arrogant?

    The accusation of "playing God" does not hold up: we accept many interventions in nature to benefit humans or achieve environmentalist goals; those who make it are not against intervening, but against benefiting animals.

    2 min
  7. 7
    Is the Proposal to Help Wild Animals Anthropocentric?

    The proposal to help wild animals is not anthropocentric: being proposed by humans does not make it human-centered; animals have no interest in having their suffering maximized so that nature can take its course.

    1 min
  8. 8
    The Less Human Influence in the World, the Better?

    "The less human influence, the better" does not hold up: if the reason is the good of animals, each case must be evaluated on its own; if it is reducing influence even when that is worse for them, it remains to be shown why influence would be wrong in itself.

    3 min
  9. 9
    Is the Proposal to Help Wild Animals Welfarist?

    Calling the proposal to help wild animals "welfarist" confuses concern for well-being with regulating exploitation; whatever one's position on regulation, it does not prevent accepting the proposal.

    4 min
  10. 10
    Does the Proposal to Help Wild Animals Violate Their Freedom?

    Helping wild animals does not violate their freedom: it is the condition for them to exercise it, since they need to be alive and free from extreme suffering; restricting them at one moment (to vaccinate them) can secure their freedom later.

    5 min
  11. 11
    The Charge of Creating Dependency

    The accusation that helping would create dependence does not apply to most interventions (which reduce births), does not take away skills animals already have, and, even if it did create dependence, it would still be better than suffering more and dying soon.

    3 min
  12. 12
    The Charge of Paternalism

    Helping would only be paternalistic if animals preferred not to be helped when facing hunger, disease, or fire, which is not the case; and even if it were paternalism, it would be the justified kind, like the kind we apply to children.

    4 min
  13. 13
    The Objection That Suffering Has Instrumental Value

    Suffering has instrumental value only when it helps the individual increase their well-being, which does not happen for most wild animals; and even when it does, it is better to get the benefit without the suffering, as with anesthesia.

    1 min
  14. 14
    Does the Proposal to Help Animals See Humans as Separate from Nature?

    It is the idea that humans should not intervene in nature that sees them as separate from it; if we are part of nature, our actions are natural too, and no one accepts for themselves the thesis that they should not protect themselves from natural processes.

    3 min
  15. 15
    If Nature Is Not Evil, Is Natural Suffering Neutral?

    Natural suffering is not neutral just because nature is not evil or responsible: what gives us reasons to prevent it is how bad it is for the one who suffers, as in the case of a child who hurts someone without being to blame.

    2 min
  16. 16
    If Nature Is Indifferent to the Suffering of Animals, Why Help Them?

    Nature being indifferent to suffering does not justify our being indifferent: ecosystems are also indifferent to human suffering; taking nature as a model means leaping from how things are to how they ought to be.

    3 min
  17. 17
    Does Respecting the Nature of Wild Animals Require Leaving Them to Fend for Themselves?

    The idea that respecting each animal's "nature" requires leaving wild animals to fend for themselves fails the impartiality test: if respecting means benefiting, leaving them to their fate harms them; if it does not, there is no reason for it to count for more than preventing harm.

    3 min
  18. 18
    Isn't It Absurd to Intervene in Conflicts Between Animals?

    Intervening in conflicts between animals is not absurd: we already intervene in predation on a large scale to protect humans, exploited animals, and environmental goals; the question is whether and how to do it for the good of the animals, without making things worse.

    4 min
  19. 19
    Which Fields Should Answer What to Do About Wild Animals?

    Describing ecosystems is the job of ecology; deciding which goals to pursue (the good of sentient beings or environmentalist values) is the job of ethics. Those who say "just apply ecology" disguise a moral position as a neutral description.

    5 min
  20. 20
    Must Environmentalists Oppose Helping Wild Animals?

    In most cases, helping wild animals does not conflict with environmentalist goals, and some forms of help achieve both; when there is a conflict, there are reasons to prioritize sentient beings, as environmentalists themselves already do in the human case.

    6 min
  21. 21
    Are We Morally Responsible Only for Human Practices?

    We are not responsible only for harms caused by humans: moral responsibility falls on decisions that determine outcomes, and the excuses ("we did not cause it," "it would exist without us," "we have no intention," "we are not close to them") do not hold up.

    10 min
  22. 22
    Attempts to Argue That Domesticated Animals Have Priority

    The arguments for prioritizing domesticated animals (historical debt, degree of dependence) do not justify denying help to wild animals: priority depends on the seriousness of the situation and the number of victims, and the dependence criterion even favors wild animals.

    4 min
  23. 23
    Would Helping Animals Prevent the Fittest Genes from Prevailing?

    Refusing to help so that "the fittest genes prevail" should be rejected: genes that are efficient at being passed on do not necessarily benefit individuals, many forms of help do not interfere with this, and the same reasoning applied to humans would be abhorrent.

    4 min
  24. 24
    Couldn't Helping Wild Animals Make Things Even Worse?

    The fear of making things worse and the appeal to ecological balance do not justify not helping: if we cannot predict the long term, we also do not know that not helping is better; the answer is more research, and balance can coexist with enormous suffering.

    6 min
  25. 25
    Objections That Question the Practicality of the Proposal to Help

    Not being able to help everyone is no reason to help no one, and what matters is the actual amount of benefit, not the percentage of the problem solved; that is why focusing on wild animals can be very effective.

    4 min
  26. 26
    What Is Already Being Done to Help Wild Animals?

    Helping wild animals already happens: there is vaccination (for example, against rabies), treatment of diseases (probiotics against mange and white-nose syndrome), rescues during disasters, and care for orphans; much more could be done.

    3 min
  27. 27
    What Else Could Be Done to Help: Establishing Welfare Biology

    Welfare biology would be an interdisciplinary field devoted to studying wild animals as sentient individuals, and not just as members of species; ideological obstacles, above all speciesism, hold back its development.

    6 min
  28. 28
    What Technologies Could Be Used to Help Animals in the Wild?

    Technologies such as individual identification of insects, population modeling, thermal imaging, drones, and data analysis can help us understand and improve the well-being of animals in the wild, and their use will expand as awareness grows.

    2 min
  29. 29
    Do Animals Have a Right Not to Be Sterilized?

    The objection that animals have a right not to be sterilized fails: there are other forms of help, respecting this supposed right would cause far more suffering to others, and the sterilization of dogs and cats is already widely accepted by animal advocates.

    10 min
  30. 30
    The Issue of Parasitism

    It is false that the proposal to prevent natural harms would forbid treating parasitism: nonsentient parasites are not harmed by death, the lives of many sentient ones would not be meaningful, and preventing new cases is not the same as killing.

    3 min
  31. 31
    The Issue of Predation

    The chapter examines the arguments about predation and concludes that there are good reasons to reduce it in the world, as long as doing so does not bring worse consequences, and that certain interventions, such as reducing primary productivity, would probably have a positive net result.

    21 min
  32. 32
    Discussing Our Ability to Assess When Life Is or Is Not Worth Living

    Saying that we cannot judge when a life is worth living does not justify not intervening: there are clear cases (extreme suffering and almost immediate death), not helping also presupposes judgments, and improving existing lives is compatible with preventing births.

    4 min
  33. 33
    The Prolonged Agony Argument

    The "prolonging the agony" argument fails: helping usually reduces suffering and extends life at the same time, since whatever causes suffering usually also causes death; and many interventions only prevent births.

    4 min
  34. 34
    The Objection That the Balance of the Situation of Animals in the Wild May Be Positive

    Even if the balance of life in nature were positive (the pleasure of a few outweighing the suffering of many), the reasons to reduce suffering would not diminish; and even for those who look only at the balance, helping those who are worst off is the most efficient way to increase it.

    4 min
  35. 35
    What Impact Does Veganism Have on the Situation of Wild Animals?

    Preventing natural harms does not mean rejecting veganism: veganism reduces the victims of exploitation and agriculture (first-degree harms), and its greatest potential lies in the ideal of consideration for all sentient beings that it helps to spread.

    5 min
  36. 36
    The Debate on the Destruction of Nature

    The chapter simulates the debate, among advocates for wild animals themselves, about "altruistic killing" (destroying habitats to reduce suffering), with arguments, replies, and rejoinders, and discusses who bears the burden of proof.

    11 min
  37. 37
    The Debate on the Expansion and Restoration of Nature

    Expanding or restoring nature tends to increase the number of animals born to suffer; the justifications (future generations, environmental values, public acceptance, human extinction) are not enough, and there are ways to address the climate crisis without creating new habitats.

    13 min
