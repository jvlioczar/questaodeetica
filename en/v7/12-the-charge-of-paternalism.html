The Charge of Paternalism

Before you read study layer, not part of the original text

Helping would only be paternalistic if animals preferred not to be helped when facing hunger, disease, or fire, which is not the case; and even if it were paternalism, it would be the justified kind, like the kind we apply to children.

Paternalism means doing something "for your own good" against your will. But a hungry animal wants to eat, and an animal in a fire wants to get out. If they run from someone who is helping, it is because they do not understand that it is help, not because they prefer to suffer.

Stopping a small child from driving is paternalism, and it is justified, because the child cannot assess the risks. Something similar happens with animals.

  1. P1 An action is paternalistic when it aims to benefit someone against their preference.
  2. P2 Animals do not prefer to remain in hunger, disease, or fire.
  3. P3 When they flee from help, it is because they do not understand the intention, not because they prefer to suffer.
  4. P4 Even if it were paternalism, it would be the justified kind, as in the case of children.
  5. C So the accusation of paternalism does not justify not helping.

The chapter’s central argument in logical form. If the premises are true, the conclusion follows. To disagree, you have to show which premise is false.

They say “We have no right to decide what is good for them.”

Answer We are deciding one way or the other: not helping is also a decision about them. And it is not hard to know that no animal prefers to starve.

Original text Luciano Carlos Cunha · Volume VII, p. 34 of the PDF edition · English translation

The proposal to help is sometimes accused of being 26, that is, of aiming to benefit the one affected, but against what they prefer27. A paternalistic action occurs, for example, when a child wants to drive a car and we stop them in order to protect them. Hence the name paternalism, since it refers to the action of parents toward their children.

Given this definition of paternalism, however, helping animals in the wild is not paternalistic. It would be only if they preferred not to be helped in situations of starvation, parasitism, disease, accidents, fires, floods, etc. Of course, there are cases in which animals flee from attempts to help them. But this does not show that they prefer not to be helped. They probably flee because they do not understand that someone is there to provide help.

Moreover, the thesis that paternalism is always unjustified can be questioned. If every paternalistic action were wrong, the only appropriate way to raise and care for children would be to do everything they wanted. But it makes sense to accuse of negligence someone who did not stop a child from driving a car, or did not give them first aid just because the child did not want it. If so, then there are paternalistic actions that are justified.

What justifies paternalism in these cases is that the child in question lacks the relevant information about how satisfying the desire in question will affect their well-being as a whole. But that is the condition of nonhuman animals as well. Therefore, even if helping them were paternalistic, it would be of the justified kind.

The Objection That the Satisfaction of Desires Should Sometimes Weigh More

It could be objected that the satisfaction of desires should sometimes weigh more than the content of positive and negative experiences. For example, suppose someone is terminally ill and all that awaits her from now on is intense suffering, but she still has one last desire to satisfy, which is very important to her. Although such a person is harmed in a certain respect if she stays alive (the suffering), she seems to be harmed

to a greater degree if she dies without fulfilling her last desire. It seems wrong to kill such a person to prevent her suffering (since she does not want to die).

However, what such an example suggests is that “there are unjustified paternalistic actions,” not that “all paternalistic actions are unjustified.” There are countless examples suggesting that paternalism is sometimes justified. For example, it is justified in the case of a child who has a lethal disease that is fortunately very easy to treat, and who refuses to take the medicine. So what we need to investigate is whether helping wild animals in nature who are victims of , if it were a paternalistic action (and, as we saw in the first response, it is not), would be justified or unjustified paternalism.

It could be suggested that the greater someone's degree of autonomy, the more unjustified paternalism is, given that the likelihood of achieving their good is greater if they make decisions for themselves (even if we determine someone's good solely on the basis of the balance between positive and negative experiences).

Even if this is true, it could not be the basis for an objection to the proposal to help animals in the wild. First, because, as we have seen, such action is not paternalistic. And even if it were, almost all nonhuman victims of natural harms have no autonomy of any kind (since they are newborns and juveniles). If the greater the autonomy, the more unjustified paternalism is, then the lesser the autonomy, the more justified paternalism is. This is why we generally consider paternalism more appropriate toward children than toward adults.

It could be objected that paternalism toward children would be justified because it would not really be paternalism, since children are not full rational agents. If so, however, then we must hold the same with respect to nonhuman animals, since they are not full rational agents either. That is, even if there were some nonhuman animal who preferred to remain in a situation in which its suffering would be increased, it would very probably not be in a favorable epistemic position to see what is best for its well-being. Therefore, even if helping it were paternalistic, it would be the justified kind of paternalism.

The proposal to help them is paternalistic. Hence, we should not help them. (VII, ch. 12).

Of course, animals sometimes flee from attempts to help them. But this does not show that they prefer not to be helped (they flee because they do not understand that someone is there to help). And even if there were some animal who preferred to remain in a situation in which its suffering would increase, it would very probably not be in an epistemic position favorable to seeing what is best for its well-being in the long term.
