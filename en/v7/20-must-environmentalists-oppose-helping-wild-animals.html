Before you read study layer, not part of the original text

In most cases, helping wild animals does not conflict with environmentalist goals, and some forms of help achieve both; when there is a conflict, there are reasons to prioritize sentient beings, as environmentalists themselves already do in the human case.

Vaccinating bees against disease helps the bees and helps nature. Many kinds of help are good for both sides. And when they are not, those who can feel pain should come first, as already happens with people.

A park can be preserved and still have a veterinary hospital. Preserving and caring are rarely either-or choices.

  1. P1 Many ways of helping animals also achieve environmentalist goals, such as vaccinating bees.
  2. P2 Others do not conflict with those goals, since they do not alter the valued properties of ecosystems.
  3. P3 When there is a conflict, only sentient beings suffer and lose experiences through death.
  4. P4 Environmentalists already accept limiting the nonbasic interests of humans, but not causing them suffering and death.
  5. C So environmentalists need not oppose helping wild animals.

The chapter’s central argument in logical form. If the premises are true, the conclusion follows. To disagree, you have to show which premise is false.

They say “Helping wild animals goes against conservation.”

Answer In most cases it does not: it only reduces the suffering of the inhabitants. When it does, the basic interests of those who can feel should take priority, as they already do in the human case.

20.1. Introduction

Respect for animals and environmentalism have very divergent foundations and goals45. value nonsentient entities, such as species and ecosystems, in themselves. Respect for animals, meanwhile, is grounded in consideration for sentient individuals, capable of suffering and enjoyment46. Because of this divergence, one might think that once someone accepts environmentalist goals, they must disagree with the proposal to help animals living in the wild, since that proposal is centered on concern for the good of individuals.

This view is mistaken for three reasons:

(1) There are some ways of helping that would also help achieve environmentalist goals.

(2) There are many ways of helping animals in the wild that, although they would not help achieve environmentalist goals, would not conflict with environmentalist goals.

(3) Even with respect to the ways of helping that would conflict with environmentalist goals, there are reasons to defend a priority of preventing the suffering and deaths of beings over those goals (and this holds even if we ought to accept environmentalist goals).

Below we will look at each of these points in detail.

20.2. Ways of Helping Animals That Would Also Achieve Environmentalist Goals

There are programs to help wild animals that could help achieve both the goal of reducing animals' suffering and deaths and environmentalist goals. One example is the vaccination of bees47. From an environmentalist point of view, vaccinating them is good

because it reduces the risk of extinction of bee species. Animal advocates, for their part, have reasons to approve of these programs because vaccination is good for the bees themselves, preventing them from getting sick and dying.

Another example is the protection of large herbivores. Environmentalists have reasons to protect them because several of their species are at risk of extinction. Animal advocates, for their part, have two strong reasons to support this kind of program. The first is that such programs reduce the suffering and deaths of the large herbivores themselves, who are then vaccinated, medicated, and given water, food, shelter, etc.48 The second, and more important, is that these animals consume a large amount of vegetation that, if it were available, would contribute to a greater number of births in those animal species whose vast majority are born only to suffer and die prematurely49. In short, protecting large herbivores can greatly reduce the total number of animals who would be born only to suffer and die prematurely.

Of course, this is a measure that must be investigated in depth, since in each population of large herbivores there could be some other factor that would make their presence increase the suffering of other animals in some other way. However, the aim here is only to show some examples of helping wild animals that both animal advocates and environmentalists could support, albeit for different reasons.

20.3. Ways of Helping Animals That Do Not Conflict with Environmentalist Goals

The proposal to help wild animals and environmentalist goals would perhaps not conflict in the vast majority of cases. For example, the different environmentalist positions on which entities are morally considerable (, , and naturocentrism) have no reason to oppose the great majority of programs to help wild animals50. We will look at this in more detail below.

Ecocentrism51 values certain ecosystems (because they are very complex, very rare, formed a long time ago, etc.). Most programs to help

animals living in the wild would neither extinguish these ecosystems nor alter the properties of theirs that ecocentrists value: they would merely mean less suffering and fewer premature deaths for their inhabitants.

Biocentrism52, in turn, values what it calls the “good of its own” of each living organism, sentient or not. That is, what biocentrism aims at is not maximizing the number of living beings, but ensuring that each existing living being can achieve its own good. Biocentrism has to accept even more interventions than proponents of a sentience-centered ethics do, since it must seek to secure the good of sentient beings and, in addition, also protect nonsentient organisms.

Finally, naturocentrism53 values only ecosystems that have not yet been altered (or have been altered very little) by human activities. Given the climate change resulting from human practices, with the exception perhaps of some ecosystems in the depths of the ocean, all ecosystems have already been considerably altered by human practices. Therefore, naturocentrism would have to accept helping animals in all these ecosystems.

Of course, what has been said above does not mean that no environmentalist position whatsoever would ever conflict with the proposal to help wild animals. For example, there could be a variant of naturocentrism according to which ecosystems that have already been transformed by human action would still have value, but would have less value the more human interference had already occurred. According to such a position, we should seek to preserve the value that remains by prohibiting new interventions, or even restore the ecosystem to its state prior to the first human intervention. This variant would therefore oppose programs to help wild animals.

However, the fact that the proposal to help wild animals conflicts with environmentalist goals in certain cases does not mean that we should reject helping animals in those cases, since there may be reasons to prioritize the good of sentient beings over environmentalist goals. This will be explained in more detail in the next section.

20.4. Reasons to Prioritize Sentient Beings over Environmentalist Goals

Attempts are sometimes made to argue against the proposal to help wild animals by claiming that nonsentient entities such as species and ecosystems have value in themselves (and not as a means of securing the good of sentient beings). The idea that there is value beyond sentience is controversial, to say the least. However, let us suppose for the sake of argument that we ought to accept environmentalist goals. This would not imply that we must therefore reject the proposal to help wild animals. This is because, even if it were shown that the entities valued in environmentalism have , this would not show that they are more important than the good of sentient beings, much less that they are the only thing that matters. We will look at these points in more detail below.

If what is relevant for someone to be morally considerable is being capable of being harmed or benefited, then we must give to all sentient beings, since sentience is what makes someone capable of being harmed or benefited, as we saw in Chapter 4. For this reason, the environmentalist view that nonsentient entities are morally considerable is already difficult to ground, and even more so the idea that sentient beings are not morally considerable, or that these entities are more important than the good of sentient beings.

For example, even if we accepted environmentalist goals, there would still be reasons, in cases of conflict, to prioritize sentient beings. One of these reasons is that, unlike the entities valued in environmentalism, sentient beings can literally be harmed and benefited, whether through negative experiences (all forms of suffering) or through being deprived of positive experiences (as happens when they die). These forms of harm are particularly serious (just consider how hard we try to avoid them ourselves), and no nonsentient entity is capable of suffering such harms.

When what is at stake is the good of humans, this is already widely accepted, including by environmentalists themselves. They generally hold that environmentalist goals are more important than certain human interests (such as economic and recreational interests), but they do not claim that we should leave humans to suffer and die as a result of . But if speciesism is unjustifiable, then there is also no justification for holding that we should not help animals who are victims of natural processes.

This chapter in Volume XII

How the author sums up this debate in The Essentials of Animal Ethics.

What matters is ecosystems and species, not animals. So, if all this suffering and death neither drives species extinct nor threatens ecosystems, we need not worry. (VII, ch. 20).
If the question of moral consideration exists because our decisions can benefit or harm those affected by them, then for someone to be morally considerable it is sufficient that they can be harmed/benefited. Every sentient being, because it is capable of experiencing certain states as positive and others as negative, can be harmed/benefited. If so, then each sentient being matters in its own right. The thesis that each sentient being matters in its own right is much less controversial than the thesis that species and ecosystems matter in themselves.
Even if animals matter, helping would conflict with environmentalist goals (such as keeping the ecosystem in certain configurations), and this makes helping wrong. (VII, ch. 20).
In the vast majority of cases there would be no conflict of goals, since programs to help wild animals would not alter the properties of ecosystems that environmentalism values: they would only mean less suffering for the inhabitants of these ecosystems. There are even several programs that would achieve both goals. For 30 example, vaccinating bees prevents an enormous number of animals from suffering from disease and also helps in the conservation of species.
But there are clearly cases in which helping would conflict with environmentalist goals. For example, environmentalists may want to keep only the native vegetation of a place, but it may be that introducing non-native vegetation would reduce the total suffering of the animals living there. In those cases, wouldn't it be wrong to help? (VII, ch. 20).
A conflict by itself is insufficient to support the conclusion that we should not help. It would have to be shown: (1) that we should abide by environmentalist goals, and (2) that these goals are more important than the basic interests of animals (such as not suffering and not dying). Even if point 1 were demonstrated, reasons could be raised for rejecting point 2. For example, it could be said that only sentient beings are harmed by suffering and death, and that these harms are particularly serious. Thus, whenever there was a conflict, there would be reasons to prioritize the basic interests of sentient beings. Environmentalists already accept this in the human case: they defend limiting the non-basic interests of humans (aesthetic, economic, recreational, etc.) to achieve their goals, but they do not hold that this justifies causing suffering and death to humans, or failing to help them when they are victims of natural processes.
