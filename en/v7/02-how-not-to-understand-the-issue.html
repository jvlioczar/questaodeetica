Before you read study layer, not part of the original text

Saying that natural harms are not a matter of ethics because no one can hold nature responsible is a mistake: the question is about what we ought to do; even saying that helping is optional is already an ethical answer.

An earthquake is not to blame for anything, but we still decide whether to help the victims. The question is not "whose fault is it?" but "what am I going to do?"

No one sues the rain for a flood, and yet a government that could save the victims and does not is held accountable. The decision belongs to whoever is able to act.

  1. P1 The argument says natural harms are not a matter of ethics because neither nature nor animals can be held responsible.
  2. P2 The question is put to us: should we help, or not?
  3. P3 Even claiming that helping is optional is an ethical position, since ethics also deals with what is permissible.
  4. C So natural harms are indeed a matter of ethics.

The chapter’s central argument in logical form. If the premises are true, the conclusion follows. To disagree, you have to show which premise is false.

They say “Nature is nature; ethics is a human thing.”

Answer Ethics is about the decisions of those who are able to decide. When animals are suffering, deciding to do nothing is also a moral decision.

2.1. The Idea That It Is Not an Ethical Issue

A common view is that harms resulting from natural disasters, hunger, thirst, disease, and conflicts between animals are not an ethical issue because it makes no sense to hold either or nonhuman animals morally responsible. According to this view, we should therefore not try to minimize this kind of harm.

There are at least two basic mistakes this view makes, explained separately below.

2.2. Whatever You Think We Should Do, It Is an Ethical Issue

The first mistake lies in the contradiction between the claims “the way natural processes affect lies outside the scope of ethical evaluation” and “we have a duty not to help wild animals who are victims of natural processes.”

But it goes further: even those who claim that helping animals in this case should be optional are also seeing the issue as an ethical one, since ethics deals not only with what is morally required or forbidden, but also with what is morally permissible but not required. In short, whatever anyone thinks we should do about this situation, it is an ethical issue simply because it concerns whether an action should be required, forbidden, or permitted.

There are questions that are not ethical questions. For example: “Is there life on other planets?”; “What was the identity of Jack the Ripper?”; “Will it rain tomorrow in Florianópolis?”; “When is Luciano 's birthday?”; “What is 2+2?”; “Is there life after death?”; “What is consciousness?” None of these questions is an ethical question, because none of them concerns how we ought to act (even though some of them may have an indirect bearing on our reasons for acting one way or another). The question of what our attitude should be toward the harms animals suffer as a result of natural processes is about how we ought to act. Therefore, it is an ethical question.

2.3. The Question Is Posed to Us, Not to Natural Processes or to Animals

The second mistake is that the fact that natural processes and nonhuman animals cannot be held morally responsible is irrelevant, since the question is not being posed to them, but to us. It is we who must answer what we should do about this issue. The fact that natural processes and animals cannot be held responsible for what they choose does not make us any less responsible.

To illustrate, imagine that the same excuse were offered in a situation involving human victims. For example, imagine that a government could save humans in a flood without sacrificing anything of greater importance in doing so. But let us imagine that the officials said: “We have no obligation to do anything, because the water cannot be held morally responsible.” At the very least, this would be regarded (rightly, in my view) as one of the flimsiest of excuses. However, the fact that the victims are nonhuman animals does not make this same excuse any less flimsy.

This chapter in Volume XII

How the author sums up this debate in The Essentials of Animal Ethics.

We should not minimize natural harms because this question lies outside the scope of ethics, since neither natural processes nor animals are accountable. (VII, ch. 2, 15, 16).
It is contradictory to claim that the question of natural harms lies outside the scope of ethics and then say that we should not minimize these harms. Moreover, the fact that natural processes and animals are not accountable is irrelevant, since the ones who have to decide are we, not they. When humans are the victims in similar situations, no one says that we should not rescue the victims just because natural processes and animals are not accountable.
Nature is neither good nor bad. Natural processes are not accountable agents. Therefore, we have no reasons to help. (VII, ch. 2, 15, 16).
It is true that natural processes are not accountable agents. But this does not mean that we have no reasons to prevent and minimize the negative effects resulting from natural processes (this is already widely accepted when humans are the victims of natural processes). Moral responsibility falls on us, in having to decide whether or not to help.
Nature is indifferent to the suffering of animals. Ecosystems arise, endure, and disappear with complete indifference to the suffering and death of animals. (VII, ch. 2, 15, 16).
Yes, but that does not justify our being indifferent as well. Ecosystems also arise, endure, and disappear with complete indifference to human suffering, but that does not justify our being indifferent to suffering. The objection in question assumes that the way natural phenomena happen should be a model for what we ought to do. In doing so, it makes an unjustified leap from describing how things are to prescribing how they ought (morally) to be.
