Before you read study layer, not part of the original text

Animal advocacy and environmentalism sometimes converge, but not always: the environment can be preserved in many configurations, and the ones environmentalism prefers often aren't the best for animals.

Animals need a place to live, but there are many ways a place can be. The way an environmentalist prefers isn't always the way in which animals suffer least.

A house can look beautiful in photos and be uncomfortable for the people who live in it. Preserving the house doesn't guarantee the residents' comfort.

  1. P1 The argument says that since animals need the environment and environmentalism preserves it, the positions converge.
  2. P2 The environment can be preserved in very different configurations.
  3. P3 Environmentalism chooses configurations by criteria like originality, rarity, and biodiversity, not by animals' well-being.
  4. C Therefore, convergence happens in some cases, but not in others.

The chapter’s central argument in logical form. If the premises are true, the conclusion follows. To disagree, you have to show which premise is false.

They say “Protecting the forest is protecting the animals.”

Answer Sometimes. But a forest can be maintained in ways that maximize births that end in suffering and death. To know what's best for animals, we have to look at their well-being, as welfare biology would.

Original text Luciano Carlos Cunha · Volume V, p. 16 of the PDF edition · English translation

One might think that, despite their opposing goals, in practice animal advocacy and always go hand in hand. For example, it is sometimes said that, since animals need the environment as a resource, and environmentalism aims to preserve the environment, animal advocates should support environmentalist measures. According to this view, the conflict is only in theory, not in practice. One problem with this view is that it fails to see that it is possible to preserve the environment in very different configurations, and that the configuration environmentalism aims at is not the best one (or even a minimally good one) for animals.

For example, suppose that modifying the natural configuration of a certain ecosystem would reduce the suffering and the number of deaths of animals from then on over time. If our concern is with the good of animals, we will support that modification. The configurations valued by environmentalism, by contrast, vary according to each environmentalist school of thought24, but none of them is centered on concern for the good of animals. For example, configurations commonly valued by environmentalist perspectives are: the one the ecosystem exhibited before the first human intervention; the one that contains only animals and plants of native species; the one that contains the rarest species; the one with the greatest degree of aesthetic properties; the one with the greatest degree of ; and so on.25 Environmentalists will advocate keeping ecosystems in these configurations even if this increases the suffering and deaths of animals over time.

For example, when evaluating which type of vegetation it is best to have in a given ecosystem, a sentience-based ethics would be guided by a criterion such as "which of them results in the least amount of suffering and premature death for the animals affected over time?". An environmentalist position, by contrast, would be guided by criteria such as "which of them is the native vegetation?", "which is rarer?", or "which increases biodiversity?", and so on, regardless of whether this is better or worse for the animals living there.

It is possible that in several cases the two will coincide. However, this will not be so in many other cases. For this reason, if our goal is the good of animals, we should not be

guided by environmentalist criteria, but rather by criteria that indicate directly how well or badly off the animals will be26. This could be informed by the field of . Welfare biology is a proposed field of research that would study animals in their ecosystems from the standpoint of what could affect them positively or negatively as individuals who have a well-being27. We will see more details about welfare biology in chapter 6.

How the author sums up this debate in The Essentials of Animal Ethics.

Despite their differences, environmentalism and animal advocacy converge in practice, since animals need the environment, and environmentalism aims to preserve it. (V, ch. 3).
This will happen in some cases, but not in others. It is possible to preserve the environment in the most diverse configurations. The configurations sought by environmentalism often do not coincide with the one that is best (or even good) for animals.
