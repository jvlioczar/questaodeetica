Do ecological balance and biodiversity coincide with what is best for animals?

Before you read study layer, not part of the original text

Ecological balance (the stability of populations) and biodiversity (the variety of species) don't measure animals' well-being; only by coincidence do they result in something better for them, and they often result in very bad situations.

“Balanced nature” means the number of animals stays about the same. But it can stay the same because almost all the babies die early. Balance doesn't mean the animals are happy.

A city can have a stable population because many people are born and many die young. A stable headcount says nothing about quality of life.

  1. P1 Ecological balance concerns the variation in population size; biodiversity, the variety of species.
  2. P2 Neither notion is based on what is best for sentient beings.
  3. P3 A population can be stable precisely because most of the young die early.
  4. C Therefore, balance and biodiversity don't indicate that animals are doing well.

The chapter’s central argument in logical form. If the premises are true, the conclusion follows. To disagree, you have to show which premise is false.

They say “Balanced nature is healthy nature for animals.”

Answer Balance is a measure of numbers, not of well-being. In most species, balance is maintained by enormous mortality among the young, that is, by a great deal of suffering.

Original text Luciano Carlos Cunha · Volume V, p. 20 of the PDF edition · English translation

As we saw in chapter 4, many programs are carried out in the name of preserving and . We also saw that several of these programs involve exterminating animals. In this chapter we will address another belief that contributes to animal advocates' acceptance of programs of this kind: the belief that ecological balance and biodiversity coincide with what is best for animals.

This belief is also one of the factors that contribute to environmentalism often being confused with consideration for animals. In short, there are people who believe that ecological balance and biodiversity coincide with what is best for animals; these people notice that one of the goals of environmentalism is to preserve ecological balance and biodiversity; and so these people conclude that environmentalism aims at what is best for animals.

The belief that ecological balance and biodiversity coincide with what is best for animals is mistaken. As explained below, a situation can have a high degree of biodiversity or ecological balance and yet be extremely bad for animals.

The notion of ecological balance concerns a certain stability relative to a state taken as a reference point33. This concept is generally used with respect to variation in

population size, but it could be used with respect to any other factor (for example, the water level of a river). The notion of biodiversity, on the other hand, concerns the variety of species. Neither of these notions is based on what is best for beings (nor does either have any direct relation to it). In other words, there are situations that are balanced and in which there is a high degree of biodiversity that can be terribly bad for animals34. We will see an example of this below.

Animals of most species have enormous numbers of offspring. Depending on the species, there are thousands or even millions of young (something common in amphibians, reptiles, fish, and invertebrates in general). In periods of approximate population stability (and therefore in balanced situations according to the definition above), it is possible to measure the rate of premature mortality from the size of the brood: if a population has remained approximately constant for several generations, then on average only one offspring per adult has survived (that is, two per brood). All the rest of the young (thousands, millions) had a short existence, usually full of suffering35. This is a balanced situation, but in it suffering and premature death are maximized. Situations in which there is biodiversity also usually show this same result.

It could be objected that, if there were less balance or less biodiversity, then suffering and premature death would be even greater. However, there is not necessarily such a correlation. Everything will depend on how a greater or lesser degree of balance or biodiversity affects other factors that directly influence the well-being of sentient beings. For example, it will depend on whether they result in a larger number of births in species most of whose members have lives full of suffering, or in species whose members have a better chance of having positive lives. It will also depend on how they affect factors such as the availability of food and shelter and the amount of disease and conflict among animals. There is no direct correlation between higher levels of ecological balance or biodiversity and the prevalence of positive lives over negative ones, or of long lives over short ones.

In short: ecological balance and biodiversity could only coincidentally result in something better for animals, and they often result in highly negative situations for them. Therefore, if the goal is to achieve the best state of affairs for animals, we should not center our analysis on the degree of biodiversity or balance, but rather investigate directly how animals are positively or negatively affected. This is something that could be done by the field of welfare biology36, which we will look at in more detail in the next chapter.

✳Author’s notes 4
  1. 33 Contrary to common belief, the notion of ecological balance is not used technically in ecology. The closest technical term would be stability. However, this term also lacks a precise definition and, for that reason, has also been abandoned among specialists. Grimm and Wissel (1997), reviewing the academic literature in ecology, found 163 distinct definitions (many of them mutually contradictory) of the term stability. Lévêque (2003, p. 224-6) points out that attempts to define it have concluded that it is too complex to be defined precisely (since it is a cluster of many other concepts) and needs to be broken down into smaller concepts, such as resilience (the capacity of an ecosystem to return to its original structure after a disturbance); elasticity (the speed of that return); amplitude (the maximum range of disturbance that still allows a return to the original state); resistance or persistence (the capacity of an ecosystem to remain constant or to show a response to environmental variations); and irreversibility (when an ecosystem cannot return to a previous state through natural processes). These concepts are a modern version of the notion of ecological balance. However, the author's conclusion is that none of these concepts can be defined precisely enough to be truly operational either. One of the reasons for this is that any point in time corresponding to a state in which an ecosystem finds itself can be taken as the reference point, and any period of time can be taken as the frequency for new measurements aimed at determining whether or not the ecosystem is stable. This implies that, depending on the point taken as the reference and on the frequency of measurement, one and the same result may or may not be considered balanced/stable, which is arbitrary. Grimm and Wissel (1997, p. 323-4) concluded that the term stability is the haziest in all of ecology, so ambiguous as to be useless, and that it could be replaced by “remains essentially unchanged,” “returns to the reference state after a disturbance,” and “the persistence over time of an ecological system,” depending on what one wants to express with the term. Moreover, the stability imagined by common sense has never been a reality in nature. On these points, see Grimm;Wissel (1997) and Lévêque (2003, p. 202-44). ↩

This chapter in Volume XII

How the author sums up this debate in The Essentials of Animal Ethics.

The greater the degree of ecological balance and biodiversity, the better for animals. That is why environmentalists aim at these things: because their goal is the good of animals. (V, ch. 5).
Environmentalism does not value ecological balance and biodiversity out of concern for the good of animals. It values these things in themselves, or as a means of keeping ecosystems in certain configurations (which are what it values in themselves). Moreover, a greater degree of balance and/or biodiversity often does not coincide with what is best (or even minimally good) for animals. These concepts are based, respectively, on variation in population size over time and on the diversity of species. This does not indicate that the well-being of animals in these situations is necessarily positive.
