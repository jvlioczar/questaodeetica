The divergence does not lie in a focus on the short or the long term

Before you read study layer, not part of the original text

The difference between the two positions isn't that one thinks short-term and the other long-term: concern for animals' future suffering is already a concern centered on them, not an environmentalist one.

Some people think environmentalists “think about the future” and animal advocates “only about right now.” That's not it. Both can think about the future. What changes is what they care about.

A doctor who avoids a treatment because it might cause problems ten years from now is still thinking about the patient. Thinking long-term doesn't change whom you care for.

  1. P1 A common belief is that environmentalism thinks long-term and animal advocacy short-term.
  2. P2 Both can consider the long term.
  3. P3 Refusing to help for fear of causing more suffering later is a concern for sentient beings.
  4. C Therefore, the disagreement is about goals, not about time horizon.

The chapter’s central argument in logical form. If the premises are true, the conclusion follows. To disagree, you have to show which premise is false.

They say “Helping wild animals is short-sighted; environmentalism thinks about the future.”

Answer Aid programs motivated by concern for animals aim precisely at less suffering and fewer deaths from then on, which is what many people mistakenly imagine environmentalism aims at.

Original text Luciano Carlos Cunha · Volume V, p. 18 of the PDF edition · English translation

Consider below two different types of interventions affecting animals in the wild28 and two common but mistaken beliefs about both.

(1) There are programs that involve killing large numbers of in the name of preserving and 29. A common view is that these interventions harm some animals now in order to prevent even greater harm to many animals in the long term.

(2) By contrast, there are proposals for helping wild animals that are focused on concern for the good of the animals themselves, and not on environmentalist goals30. A common view is that these proposals benefit some animals now while neglecting the risk that this will further increase suffering and death in the long term.

According to these two common beliefs, both perspectives share the same goal (benefiting animals), and the difference between them would be that one takes into account the long-term impact of actions and the other does not. We will see why both beliefs are mistaken if we look at which criteria animal advocates and environmentalists would use to evaluate how good or bad the complete history of the world would be31. In this way, we will avoid any confusion that might arise about what each of these perspectives aims at in the short and the long term.

Animal advocates will evaluate how good or bad the complete history of the world is on the basis of how beings are positively or negatively affected. Environmentalist perspectives, by contrast, will evaluate it according to the degree to which ecosystems exhibit characteristics such as complexity, diversity, rarity, and aesthetic properties, how well they represent certain ideals, whether or not they contain only members of native species,

how little they have been transformed by humans, and so on.32 In environmentalism these characteristics are valued intrinsically (and not instrumentally, to secure the good of animals).

This means that environmentalism would say that, if a complete history of the world exhibits ecosystems in those configurations to a greater degree, then it is better, even if it is much worse for animals (for example, even if it contains much more suffering and premature death, fewer positive lives, and so on).

Accordingly, the goal of environmentalist practices of exterminating wild animals is not to prevent more suffering and premature death from occurring from then on. The goal is to ensure that ecosystems exhibit those properties, even if this increases the amount of suffering and death from then on.

On the other hand, in programs to help wild animals that are motivated by concern for the animals themselves, what is aimed at is precisely to ensure that, from then on, the best history of the world for the animals affected comes about. Therefore, the belief that the proposal to help wild animals helps some animals now and neglects the long-term impact of such actions is also mistaken.

In short, programs to help wild animals that are motivated by concern for the good of the animals themselves aim at exactly what common sense mistakenly believes environmentalism aims at: that there be less suffering and premature death from then on. This mistaken belief about what each of these perspectives aims at is one of the factors that lead many people to reject the proposal to help animals and to accept environmentalist measures.

This chapter in Volume XII

How the author sums up this debate in The Essentials of Animal Ethics.

Animal advocacy defends helping wild animals without worrying about the consequences, and environmentalism prescribes not helping them for fear that doing so could cause even more suffering. (V, ch. 4).
The difference between these positions has nothing to do with one of them considering the long term and the other not. Both can consider the long term. What distinguishes them is their goals. Claiming that a certain form of help should not be carried out because it could cause more suffering in the long term is a concern for sentient beings (and is therefore not an environmentalist concern).
