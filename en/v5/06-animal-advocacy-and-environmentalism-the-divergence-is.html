Animal advocacy and environmentalism: the divergence is ethical, not scientific

Before you read study layer, not part of the original text

The disagreement between animal advocacy and environmentalism is ethical, not scientific: ecology describes and doesn't prescribe goals, and a position centered on animals' good can be as scientifically informed as any other, for example through welfare biology.

Science helps us understand how nature works. But it doesn't tell us what we should want. People who want to help animals can use the same science as environmentalists, just for a different goal.

Chemistry explains how to make medicines and poisons. Choosing to make medicines is an ethical decision, not a chemical one.

  1. P1 Ecology describes how ecosystems work.
  2. P2 Which goals to pursue, such as animals' good or environmentalist values, is an ethical question.
  3. P3 Ecological knowledge can be used for any of these goals, including through welfare biology.
  4. C Therefore, animal advocacy can be as scientifically informed as environmentalism.

The chapter’s central argument in logical form. If the premises are true, the conclusion follows. To disagree, you have to show which premise is false.

They say “Animal advocates are sentimental; environmentalists follow the science.”

Answer Science doesn't choose goals. Saying it “tells us” to preserve species is dressing up a moral position as scientific neutrality.

Original text Luciano Carlos Cunha · Volume V, p. 23 of the PDF edition · English translation

need to draw on knowledge from ecology and other related sciences to achieve their goals. Because of this, there is an idea that, in order to use the existing knowledge in these fields and to obtain new knowledge in them, one must adopt an environmentalist stance. According to this view, programs aimed at helping wild animals, if their goal is to secure the good of the animals themselves37 (and not, indirectly, an environmentalist goal), could not be scientifically informed.

This idea is mistaken. Ecology deals with categories such as species and ecosystems, but this does not imply that, in order to obtain knowledge in ecology or to use existing knowledge, it is necessary to incorporate a perspective that values entities such as species or ecosystems intrinsically, rather than individuals. Scientific, descriptive knowledge is one thing; the normative goals that guide the use of that knowledge and the search for new knowledge are another38.

It is not only interventions with environmentalist goals that can be well informed scientifically. To think so is to confuse ecology with environmentalism. Ecology is the science that describes how interactions take place in ecosystems. Environmentalism, on the other hand, is the normative view holding that what has are nonsentient entities such as species and ecosystems, and not sentient beings. Scientific knowledge is descriptive and, as such, can be used by the most varied normative positions to achieve different goals. Environmentalism is only one of these positions.

Knowledge from ecology and related fields is predominantly used to achieve and environmentalist goals. However, it could equally be used in an attempt to achieve the goal of reducing the suffering and deaths of animals. Indeed, much more knowledge in these fields would be sought if animals received , since there would then be an interest in finding out how animals are positively or negatively affected by what happens in ecosystems (something that does not happen – or happens to a much lesser extent – if the good of animals is not valued

intrinsically). This is one of the reasons why the creation of the field of welfare biology39 is being proposed. We will see more about this field below.

focuses on investigating how animals in their ecosystems are positively and negatively affected as sentient beings who have a well-being. It is therefore different from conservation biology40, which studies animals as components of ecosystems or specimens of species. It is also different from compassionate conservation41, which studies animals from the standpoint of conservation while avoiding harming them in that pursuit. Finally, it is also different from conservation welfare42, which investigates how to promote the well-being of animals when conservation measures are implemented. Knowledge from these other fields can be useful to welfare biology (and vice versa), but the focus of each of these fields is different. What essentially distinguishes welfare biology from all these other fields is that it studies the way animals are positively or negatively affected as sentient beings who have a well-being, regardless of whether or not this serves conservation purposes.

Welfare biology would bring together knowledge from several other disciplines, such as ecology, animal welfare science, zoology, ethology, and veterinary science. It is a crossdisciplinary field of research, which is something different from interdisciplinary and transdisciplinary fields. This will be explained in more detail below.

With regard to their disciplinarity, types of research are usually classified as43: (1) intradisciplinary (when research is carried out within a single discipline); (2) multidisciplinary (when several disciplines offer different perspectives on a problem); (3) interdisciplinary (when knowledge and methods from several disciplines are integrated and synthesized); (4) transdisciplinary (when a framework is created that goes beyond the various disciplines involved); and (5) crossdisciplinary (when one or more disciplines are viewed from the perspective of another). Welfare biology is crossdisciplinary because each discipline involved would be conducted with a focus on

investigating how animals are positively or negatively affected as sentient beings who have a well-being.

Everything we have seen above clearly indicates that the normative perspective that intrinsically values the good of animals as sentient beings (and, consequently, the goal of preventing and minimizing their suffering and premature deaths) can be as scientifically well informed as any other normative perspective.

How the author sums up this debate in The Essentials of Animal Ethics.

Environmentalism is scientifically informed by ecology, whereas animal advocacy is anti-scientific. (V, ch. 6, VII, ch. 19).
In fact, any normative view needs to be scientifically well informed since, otherwise, it will not achieve the goals it pursues. Ecology is a science that describes how interactions in ecosystems take place. This knowledge can be used in an attempt to pursue environmentalist goals, but it can also be used in an attempt to benefit sentient beings.
