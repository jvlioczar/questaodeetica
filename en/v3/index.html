Volume III · Intermediate level

Animals and the Harm of Death

An inquiry into the harm of death and its magnitude

13 chaptersabout 89 min of reading71 notes46 references

0 of 13 read

About this volume

This book presents the main arguments in the debate on the harm of death for nonhuman animals. It investigates what the conditions are for death to be a harm and also what criteria we should use to assess the magnitude of the harm of death.

ISBN 978-65-01-35507-8 · 1st edition, February 2025. Original text by Luciano Carlos Cunha, translated into English.

In a nutshell

Is killing painlessly wrong? This volume investigates what the harm of death consists of, why it does not depend on understanding what dying is, and examines attempts to claim that death harms animals only a little: lack of intellectual pleasures, weak psychological connectedness, replaceability, short lifespans.

For kids

If someone blows out your candle too early, you lose all the good things you were still going to live, even if it does not hurt. It is the same with animals: dying young takes away the good days that were still to come.

You will learn

  • Why death is a harm even when it is painless
  • How to measure the size of the harm of death (QALYs)
  • Why animals are not "replaceable"
  • The difference between the harm of death and the wrongness of killing

It helps to have read: Volumes I and II.

Chapters

  1. 1
    What Does the Harm of Death Consist Of?

    Against the Epicurean view that death is not a harm, the chapter argues that it harms by preventing the good things the individual would have lived; and even if Epicurus were right, this would apply equally to humans and animals.

    5 min
  2. 2
    Discussing the Claim That Animals Are Not Harmed by Death

    Animals are harmed by death even without understanding what it is and without desires about the future: every sentient being with the prospect of a minimally meaningful life ahead loses something by dying.

    6 min
  3. 3
    Objections to the Account of the Harm of Death as the Prevention of What Is Positive

    The chapter examines three objections to the idea that death is a deprivation harm (the epistemological problem, the temporal asymmetry, and the problem of potential beings) and shows that they don't overturn the account.

    4 min
  4. 4
    Are Those Incapable of Intellectual Pleasures Only Slightly Harmed by Death?

    Saying that those without intellectual pleasures are only slightly harmed by death doesn't hold up: intellectual pleasures aren't necessarily more valuable, and even if they were, that wouldn't make other pleasures of little value.

    2 min
  5. 5
    How Can We Assess How Much Someone Is Harmed by Death?

    The size of the harm of death depends on how much lifetime the individual loses and on the quality of that time, which can be expressed in QALYs; there are also reasons to take into account what they have already lived.

    8 min
  6. 6
    Objections to Assessing the Magnitude of the Harm of Death on the Basis of QALYs

    The objection that measuring the harm of death by QALYs has absurd implications appeals to intuitions that may be biased; until another criterion is grounded independently, we have reasons to use QALYs.

    7 min
  7. 7
    Do Animals Have Weaker Psychological Connectedness?

    Even if psychological connectedness to the future mattered for the harm of death, simpler minds may have proportionally greater connectedness, since their contents vary less; and strong connectedness doesn't even seem necessary for being greatly harmed.

    3 min
  8. 8
    Discussing the Claim That Animals Are Replaceable

    The idea that animals are replaceable, that is, that killing one isn't wrong if another with an equally good life is born in its place, fails: it would permit parents to replace their children, and it confuses being qualitatively “the same” with being the same individual.

    5 min
  9. 9
    Are Nonhuman Animals Only Slightly Harmed by Death?

    With examples like Bia's trip to the pyramids, the chapter shows that the strength of our reasons to pursue what's good and avoid what's bad doesn't depend on psychological connectedness; so connectedness doesn't show that animals are only slightly harmed by death.

    9 min
  10. 10
    The Claim That Having Little Time Left Makes Death a Small Harm

    Having little lifetime left, whether from old age or from belonging to a short-lived species, doesn't make death a small harm; and animals with smaller brains may even perceive time more slowly.

    12 min
  11. 11
    What If Humans Were More Harmed by Death?

    Even supposing humans were more harmed by death, that wouldn't justify killing animals for lesser benefits, or killing some to save others, or ignoring the number of victims.

    7 min
  12. 12
    Differentiated Moral Status, the Harm of Death, and the Wrongness of Killing

    The wrongness of killing and the reason to save lives should depend on the magnitude of the harm of death; the “two-tier” approach, which gives a privileged status to certain individuals, fails the impartiality test.

    14 min
  13. 13
    Further Reflections: Death, Annihilation, Rational Decision, and Probabilities

    Considering the possibility of life after death doesn't radically change the assessment: there's uncertainty about it and about what it would be like, while we know how the current life would probably go on; so it doesn't justify killing those who have a good life.

    7 min
