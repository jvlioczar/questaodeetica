Intermediate level

Death, environmentalism, the sentience criterion, activism and biases.

How to study this level

  • Read the “In one sentence” layer first, then the original text.
  • Answer the questions in “Practice” without going back to the text; then check your answers.
  • At the end of each volume, take the volume quiz.

Volume III · Animals and the Harm of Death

  1. 1
    What Does the Harm of Death Consist Of?

    Against the Epicurean view that death is not a harm, the chapter argues that it harms by preventing the good things the individual would have lived; and even if Epicurus were right, this would apply equally to humans and animals.

    5 min
  2. 2
    Discussing the Claim That Animals Are Not Harmed by Death

    Animals are harmed by death even without understanding what it is and without desires about the future: every sentient being with the prospect of a minimally meaningful life ahead loses something by dying.

    6 min
  3. 3
    Objections to the Account of the Harm of Death as the Prevention of What Is Positive

    The chapter examines three objections to the idea that death is a deprivation harm (the epistemological problem, the temporal asymmetry, and the problem of potential beings) and shows that they don't overturn the account.

    4 min
  4. 4
    Are Those Incapable of Intellectual Pleasures Only Slightly Harmed by Death?

    Saying that those without intellectual pleasures are only slightly harmed by death doesn't hold up: intellectual pleasures aren't necessarily more valuable, and even if they were, that wouldn't make other pleasures of little value.

    2 min
  5. 5
    How Can We Assess How Much Someone Is Harmed by Death?

    The size of the harm of death depends on how much lifetime the individual loses and on the quality of that time, which can be expressed in QALYs; there are also reasons to take into account what they have already lived.

    8 min
  6. 6
    Objections to Assessing the Magnitude of the Harm of Death on the Basis of QALYs

    The objection that measuring the harm of death by QALYs has absurd implications appeals to intuitions that may be biased; until another criterion is grounded independently, we have reasons to use QALYs.

    7 min
  7. 7
    Do Animals Have Weaker Psychological Connectedness?

    Even if psychological connectedness to the future mattered for the harm of death, simpler minds may have proportionally greater connectedness, since their contents vary less; and strong connectedness doesn't even seem necessary for being greatly harmed.

    3 min
  8. 8
    Discussing the Claim That Animals Are Replaceable

    The idea that animals are replaceable, that is, that killing one isn't wrong if another with an equally good life is born in its place, fails: it would permit parents to replace their children, and it confuses being qualitatively “the same” with being the same individual.

    5 min
  9. 9
    Are Nonhuman Animals Only Slightly Harmed by Death?

    With examples like Bia's trip to the pyramids, the chapter shows that the strength of our reasons to pursue what's good and avoid what's bad doesn't depend on psychological connectedness; so connectedness doesn't show that animals are only slightly harmed by death.

    9 min
  10. 10
    The Claim That Having Little Time Left Makes Death a Small Harm

    Having little lifetime left, whether from old age or from belonging to a short-lived species, doesn't make death a small harm; and animals with smaller brains may even perceive time more slowly.

    12 min
  11. 11
    What If Humans Were More Harmed by Death?

    Even supposing humans were more harmed by death, that wouldn't justify killing animals for lesser benefits, or killing some to save others, or ignoring the number of victims.

    7 min
  12. 12
    Differentiated Moral Status, the Harm of Death, and the Wrongness of Killing

    The wrongness of killing and the reason to save lives should depend on the magnitude of the harm of death; the “two-tier” approach, which gives a privileged status to certain individuals, fails the impartiality test.

    14 min
  13. 13
    Further Reflections: Death, Annihilation, Rational Decision, and Probabilities

    Considering the possibility of life after death doesn't radically change the assessment: there's uncertainty about it and about what it would be like, while we know how the current life would probably go on; so it doesn't justify killing those who have a good life.

    7 min

Volume VI · To Whom Do We Owe Moral Consideration?

  1. 1
    What is relevant for knowing to whom we owe consideration?

    A criterion is justified only if it's based on a distinction relevant to the question at hand; to decide whom to give consideration, what's relevant is being able to be harmed or benefited, which leads to the criterion of sentience.

    9 min
  2. 2
    Is the criterion of sentience anthropocentric at bottom?

    The sentience criterion isn't anthropocentric: it doesn't consider animals because they resemble humans, but because they can be harmed and benefited, which reason or language doesn't determine.

    1 min
  3. 3
    Does the criterion of sentience discriminate against those who do not belong to the animal kingdom?

    The sentience criterion isn't “kingdomism”: it doesn't include all animals (sponges probably don't feel) or exclude other kingdoms on principle; it includes any sentient being, even a non-organic one, should one come to exist.

    2 min
  4. 4
    According to the criterion of sentience, is suffering the only problem?

    The sentience criterion doesn't recognize only suffering as harm: a sentient being is also harmed by the absence of what would be positive for them, as in death.

    2 min
  5. 5
    If sentience is what matters, why are there harms that do not affect experiences?

    Harms that don't alter one's experiences, such as violating someone who is unconscious, may be an objection to hedonism, but not to the sentience criterion, which is also compatible with harms to actual or informed preferences.

    3 min
  6. 6
    Does the criterion of sentience exclude insects?

    The sentience criterion doesn't exclude insects: there's evidence of sentience in several species, and the rest have barely been studied; if there's someone in those bodies, they deserve the same consideration as any sentient being.

    4 min
  7. 7
    Does accepting the criterion of sentience imply assuming that values are subjective?

    Accepting the sentience criterion doesn't imply that values are subjective: there's a confusion between “subjective” in the sense of referring to consciousness and in the sense of there being no objective truth.

    2 min
  8. 8
    Are animals with simpler brains less sentient?

    There are no good reasons to say that animals with simpler brains are less sentient or deserve less: they're as much a “someone” as any sentient being, their experiences may even be more intense at certain stages, and giving less weight because of cognition is intelligence-based discrimination.

    8 min
  9. 9
    If there is a need for environmental ethics, is the criterion of sentience wrong?

    The existence of an environmental ethics doesn't show that non-sentient entities have intrinsic value: we also need an ethics of income distribution, and income has no intrinsic value. An “ethics of environmental management” may be enough.

    3 min
  10. 10
    Isn't a World with Less Suffering, but Managed by Humans, Absurd?

    The idea that a world with less suffering but managed by humans would be worse reveals a prejudice: an ecosystem indistinguishable from a natural one and better for sentient beings wouldn't be worse just because of human influence.

    3 min
  11. 11
    If Sentient Beings Should Be Respected, Does Nature Have Intrinsic Value?

    Respecting sentient beings doesn't imply that nature has intrinsic value: what makes sentient beings considerable (being able to be affected) isn't present in nature as a whole; confusing “nature” with “animals” produces this mistake.

    2 min
  12. 12
    Does the Sentience Criterion Imply Accepting Environmentalist Goals?

    The fact that some sentient beings have an interest in nature taking its course doesn't imply accepting environmentalist goals: considering an interest isn't the same as having to fulfill it, and fulfilling it here would cause suffering and death to an enormous number of others.

    2 min
  13. 13
    Do Species Have Interests?

    Species don't have interests: they're abstract entities, categories created to group similar living beings; those who can be benefited or harmed are the sentient individuals who make them up.

    4 min
  14. 14
    If Some Nonsentient Entity Had Intrinsic Value, What Would Follow?

    Even if non-sentient entities had intrinsic value, there would be strong reasons to prioritize sentient beings in conflicts: only they can suffer, and only for them does death prevent positive experiences.

    3 min
  15. 15
    Is Biocentrism Better Because It Includes a Greater Number of Beings?

    A broader criterion isn't necessarily better: it would have to be shown that the beings included can actually be harmed; and biocentrism excludes possible non-living sentient beings, discriminating against them.

    3 min
  16. 16
    Sentience and Life: What Has Value as an End and What Has Value as a Means?

    That being alive is necessary for being sentient doesn't give life intrinsic value; it's the other way around: biological life has value as a means to meaningful experiences, not sentience as a means to life.

    4 min
  17. 17
    Can Nonsentient Living Beings Be Harmed?

    Non-sentient living beings don't seem capable of being harmed: everyday expressions like “the plant is happy” don't show that there's someone there for whom things go well or badly.

    3 min
  18. 18
    Does the Behavior of Certain Plants and Fungi Show That They Value Certain Things?

    Behaviors of plants and fungi (growing toward light, closing leaves when touched) can be explained as physicochemical reactions; without a structure that gives rise to experiences, behavior alone doesn't indicate sentience.

    5 min
  19. 19
    Does Life Without Sentience Have Value?

    Life without sentience doesn't seem to have value: examples like total irreversible coma, the fraudulent insurer, and sentience without life show that what we value is experiences, not biological life.

    4 min
  20. 20
    Does Every Living Being Have “Biological Interests”?

    Non-sentient living beings don't have “biological interests” in the moral sense: in sentient beings, satisfying biological needs is only a means to well-being; having needs isn't enough to have interests.

    3 min
  21. 21
    If Plants Are Morally Considerable, Does It Make No Difference Whether We Eat Animals or Plants?

    If plants were morally considerable, it wouldn't follow that it makes no difference whether we eat animals or plants: animals suffer and consume many plants; the implication would be fruitarianism, and even Paul Taylor recognizes the importance of suffering.

    2 min

Volume V · Differences Between Environmentalism and Consideration for Animals

  1. 1
    Respect for animals and environmentalism: complementary or opposed?

    Animal advocacy and environmentalism start from opposite foundations: one values the individuals who feel; the other, non-sentient entities like species and ecosystems. That's why there are environmentalist practices that exterminate animals.

    6 min
  2. 2
    The divergence concerns ends, not only means

    The difference between environmentalism and animal advocacy lies in the ends, not just the means: considering individuals isn't always preferring one over many, and considering “the whole” isn't considering the majority, but abstract entities.

    5 min
  3. 3
    Despite their differences, do animal advocacy and environmentalism converge in practice?

    Animal advocacy and environmentalism sometimes converge, but not always: the environment can be preserved in many configurations, and the ones environmentalism prefers often aren't the best for animals.

    2 min
  4. 4
    The divergence does not lie in a focus on the short or the long term

    The difference between the two positions isn't that one thinks short-term and the other long-term: concern for animals' future suffering is already a concern centered on them, not an environmentalist one.

    3 min
  5. 5
    Do ecological balance and biodiversity coincide with what is best for animals?

    Ecological balance (the stability of populations) and biodiversity (the variety of species) don't measure animals' well-being; only by coincidence do they result in something better for them, and they often result in very bad situations.

    3 min
  6. 6
    Animal advocacy and environmentalism: the divergence is ethical, not scientific

    The disagreement between animal advocacy and environmentalism is ethical, not scientific: ecology describes and doesn't prescribe goals, and a position centered on animals' good can be as scientifically informed as any other, for example through welfare biology.

    3 min
  7. 7
    Why is respect for animals confused with environmentalism?

    Several factors explain the confusion between animal advocacy and environmentalism, such as the belief that both want what's good for animals and the confusion between environmentalism and ecology; it leads animal advocates to support practices that seriously harm animals.

    7 min
  8. 8
    What do environmentalist extermination programs aim at?

    Environmentalist programs to exterminate “invasive” species don't aim to reduce animals' suffering and deaths (sometimes they greatly increase them): they aim to maintain valued configurations. They fail the impartiality test.

    4 min
  9. 9
    Is environmentalism anthropocentric?

    Most environmentalists are anthropocentric, since they don't apply to humans what they apply to animals; a rare few, like Linkola, would apply it to humans too. There are reasons to reject both positions.

    3 min
  10. 10
    Questioning environmentalist assumptions

    It hasn't been shown that non-sentient entities (species, ecosystems) have intrinsic value, let alone that they're worth more than sentient beings; even if they did, that wouldn't justify violating basic interests such as not suffering and not dying.

    8 min
  11. 11
    Why are animal advocates reluctant to acknowledge the divergence?

    Animal advocates are reluctant to acknowledge their disagreement with environmentalism for fear of opposing it and because it's hard to revise long-held beliefs and practices; these are the same biases they ask others to overcome.

    6 min

Volume VIII · Effective Activism

  1. 1
    How to Choose Which Causes and Problems to Prioritize and Which Strategies Are Best?

    To choose causes, problems, and strategies, we can use criteria: how serious the situation is (the number of victims and how bad it is for each one), tractability, the size of the possible benefit, long-term effects, tackling causes and not just symptoms, and the degree of neglect.

    15 min
  2. 2
    How Does the Animal Cause Fare on the Priority Criteria?

    By the criteria for prioritizing (seriousness, number of victims, neglect, and tractability), the animal cause does very well; the number of victims is so large that even people who give less weight to animals would have to prioritize it.

    8 min
  3. 3
    Among the Problems Affecting Animals, Which Are the Most Important?

    Within the animal cause, the most neglected and serious problems are animals raised for food (especially aquatic animals and invertebrates), animals in the wild who are victims of natural processes, and the future; that is why this is where we can make the most difference.

    7 min
  4. 4
    The Use of Anthropocentric and Environmentalist Arguments in the Animal Cause

    Using only anthropocentric or environmentalist arguments (health, the environment) to defend animals carries risks: different motivations have different consequences, they may reinforce the idea that harm to animals is not enough on its own, and they may get in the way of concern for bigger problems, such as wild animals and the future.

    7 min
  5. 5
    The Total Approach and the Sufficient Approach

    The total approach defends a complete ethical view; the sufficient approach shows that certain goals (such as consideration for all sentient beings) can be accepted from very different views. It is important to combine the two.

    13 min
  6. 6
    Shifting the Focus in Debates: Why Is Speciesism Rarely Discussed?

    In debates, people who defend practices that harm animals often shift the focus to health, the environment, or practical details; accepting this shift prevents any discussion of speciesism, and without that discussion the situation of animals is unlikely to change.

    14 min
  7. 7
    Moral Beliefs, Motivation to Act, and the Role of Ethical Reflection

    The case of animal ethics experts who consume animals raises the question of moral motivation: perhaps they do not think it is wrong, think it is only slightly wrong, are not sincere, have a weak will, or decide not to act; no form of persuasion escapes these limits.

    6 min
  8. 8
    Normative Objections Disguised as Strategic Objections

    Sometimes people who disagree with a goal (a normative objection) present their disagreement as a strategic doubt ("it would be great, but it will never work"); the burden of proof lies with whoever makes such claims, and there are reasons to be suspicious of them.

    17 min
  9. 9
    Focusing on Veganism versus Focusing on Reducing Consumption

    The debate between focusing on veganism or on reducing consumption is, in general, about strategy and not about the goal, which is shared; there are arguments on both sides, and they need to be evaluated without deciding in advance by intuition.

    10 min
  10. 10
    Why the Term Welfarism Is Highly Ambiguous

    "Welfarism" has at least seven meanings, which concern different debates; accepting or rejecting one of the positions called by that name does not imply accepting or rejecting the others.

    4 min
  11. 11
    The Debate on Regulating Animal Exploitation

    The debate about regulating exploitation involves two causal questions: whether reforms lead to abolition and whether they reduce suffering; there are arguments on both sides, and reforms should be compared with other strategies, such as cultivated meat and antispeciesist education.

    22 min
  12. 12
    The Debate on Cultivated Meat

    Cultivated meat could greatly reduce exploitation; the chapter examines objections from defenders of conventional meat and from animal advocates, and concludes that it has great potential, including, in the future, for reducing natural harms.

    34 min

Volume X · Biases and Their Influence

  1. 1
    Biases That Lead Us to Neglect the Problems with the Most Victims

    Biases are psychological tendencies that systematically lead to faulty reasoning; several of them (the identifiable victim effect, scope neglect, the collapse of compassion) make the problems with the most victims precisely the most neglected ones.

    12 min
  2. 2
    Biases That Incline Us to Neglect the Situation of Wild Animals

    Several biases lead us to neglect wild animals: status quo bias, a preference for certain kinds of animals, the number of victims, the belief that nature is good (as in the just-world hypothesis), attempts to justify suffering, doubts about feasibility, and underestimating the public.

    28 min
  3. 3
    Biases That Contribute to the Neglect of the Long Term and of S-Risks

    Biases such as temporal bias, difficulty dealing with probabilities and expected value, and overconfidence in intuitions contribute to neglecting the long term and s-risks; the answer is to keep an open mind about counterintuitive questions.

    14 min
  4. 4
    Cognitive Biases and Their Influence on Our Decisions That Affect Animals

    Many biases make it hard to revise our beliefs and strategies about animals: status quo bias, system justification, moral licensing, the affect heuristic, the mere-exposure effect, the sunk cost fallacy, illusory superiority, the bias blind spot, the illusory truth effect, authority bias, and others.

    30 min
  5. 5
    Motivated Reasoning and Its Influence on the Evaluation of Arguments

    Motivated reasoning means judging arguments with different levels of rigor depending on whether we like the conclusion; in addition, many people raise objections they do not believe in, as excuses; this explains why attempts to justify speciesism are rarely examined rigorously.

    4 min
  6. 6
    Some Biases That Can Influence the Choice of Strategies and How the Public Receives Them

    Activists are also affected by biases when choosing strategies (negativity, choice overload, nostalgia, order effects), and so is the public when receiving the message; presenting the arguments for considering animals first can change how the rest is received.

    15 min
