Before you read study layer, not part of the original text

More complex cognition doesn't always mean suffering more: sometimes it increases suffering (anticipation), sometimes it reduces it (understanding that a treatment is for your own good); and lesser cognition usually comes with greater vulnerability.

An adult getting a shot knows the poke is quick and good for them. A kitten taken to the vet doesn't understand anything and can get terrified. Sometimes the one who understands less suffers more.

A passenger who knows turbulence is normal stays calm; another, who knows nothing about planes, panics. Knowing more can either calm you down or scare you.

  1. P1 The thesis says humans always suffer more because they have more sophisticated cognition.
  2. P2 In many situations, those who understand less suffer more, because they don't grasp what's happening.
  3. P3 Moreover, lower cognitive capacity usually increases vulnerability.
  4. P4 And even if someone suffered less, that wouldn't justify disregarding them.
  5. C Therefore, a higher degree of cognition doesn't justify the treatment given to animals.

The chapter’s central argument in logical form. If the premises are true, the conclusion follows. To disagree, you have to show which premise is false.

They say “Humans suffer more because they understand what's happening to them.”

Answer Sometimes; sometimes it's the other way around. An animal caught to be given medicine may panic, thinking he's going to die. And suffering a little less would never justify suffering for nothing.

Original text Luciano Carlos Cunha · Volume II, p. 32 of the PDF edition · English translation

It is sometimes argued that prioritizing humans is consistent with equal consideration. The reason for this would be that, because of their more sophisticated cognitive capacities, humans would suffer more intensely. For example, they are capable of suffering from anticipating and remembering negative events.

It is true that greater cognitive capacity sometimes entails greater suffering. For example, only someone who understands that they will be killed a week from now is capable of suffering in anticipation. Only someone who remembers violence they suffered a few decades ago is capable of suffering through recollection. However, in many other cases it is lesser cognitive capacity that entails greater suffering. For example, nonhuman animals and young children typically suffer more when given an injection or medication precisely because they do not understand that they are being helped by it. Moreover, beings with greater cognitive capacities can also have their present suffering relieved by remembering or anticipating positive events, something impossible for beings who lack this capacity36.

Moreover, lesser cognitive capacity usually comes with greater vulnerability. That is why babies or adults with a cognitive impairment need much more care than normal adults do.

Of course, there may be cases in which someone has greater cognitive capacity and also greater vulnerability. For example, someone with the mental faculties of a normal adult human who is quadriplegic is more vulnerable than a young child. However, if, in addition to being quadriplegic, they also had reduced cognitive capacities, they would be even more vulnerable. In other words, even though a lower degree of cognitive capacity does not by itself determine someone's level of vulnerability, it always contributes to increasing it.

Therefore, greater cognitive capacity does not always entail greater harm. In fact, given the relationship between lesser cognitive capacity and vulnerability, in most cases exactly the opposite is true.

This chapter in Volume XII

How the author sums up this debate in The Essentials of Animal Ethics.

Humans are capable of suffering to a greater degree, owing to their high degree of cognition. Therefore, the standard treatment given to nonhuman animals is justified. (II, ch. 9).
Even if this were the case, the fact that one kind of individual suffers to a greater degree does not imply that it is justified to disregard the individuals capable of suffering to a lesser degree. Moreover, it is also possible to question the thesis that a higher degree of cognition implies greater suffering. This will be so in certain situations (for example, only someone who understands that they will be killed in a few days will suffer in anticipation), but not in others (for example, an animal that is captured to be given medication and panics because it thinks it will be killed). Furthermore, a lower degree of cognition usually comes with greater vulnerability, which gives us reasons to give these 4 individuals greater protection.
