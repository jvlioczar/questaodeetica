Volume II · Basic level

Considering Sentient Beings

Foundations of a sentience-centered ethics

13 chaptersabout 52 min of reading62 notes104 references

0 of 13 read

About this volume

This book presents the main arguments that ground full moral consideration for all sentient beings, that is, for all beings capable of suffering and enjoyment. It also examines the main objections to this proposal, as well as its practical implications, and investigates what criteria we could use to determine which beings are sentient.

ISBN 978-65-01-30511-0 · . Original text by Luciano Carlos Cunha, translated into English.

In a nutshell

If species is not what matters, what is? This volume presents the most widely accepted answer in contemporary animal ethics: sentience. It explains the argument from relevance, the argument from species overlap, the principle of equal consideration, and practical tests for noticing when we are being biased.

For kids

Anyone who can feel pain and joy can be hurt or made happy. That is why everyone who feels deserves care, whether it is a person, a dog, a fish or a bee. The book teaches you to ask: "What if I were in their place?"

You will learn

  • What sentience is and which beings probably have it
  • The argument from relevance and the argument from species overlap
  • The principle of equal consideration and the impartiality test
  • What to do when we are not sure whether a being can feel

It helps to have read: Volume I helps, but it is not required.

Chapters

  1. 1
    What Is Relevant to Knowing to Whom We Owe Consideration?

    What matters in deciding whom we owe consideration is who can be harmed or benefited by our decisions; the capacity to act morally matters for holding someone responsible, not for considering them.

    6 min
  2. 2
    What Is the Argument from Species Overlap?

    If the lack of certain abilities and relationships in some humans doesn't justify treating them worse, the same lack in animals doesn't either; and it's usually a reason to care more, since it increases vulnerability.

    2 min
  3. 3
    What Is Sentience and Why Does It Matter?

    Sentience is the capacity to have positive and negative experiences; it's what makes someone a “someone” rather than a “something,” and so able to be harmed by the presence of what's bad or the absence of what's good.

    2 min
  4. 4
    Which Beings Are Sentient?

    To find out which beings are sentient, we look at evidence: a centralized nervous system, other physiological indicators, overall behavior, and evolutionary advantage. Many invertebrates meet these criteria, and non-organic sentience is a future possibility.

    5 min
  5. 5
    Why Equal Consideration?

    The principle of equal consideration says that harms and benefits of similar magnitude should have the same weight, whoever they belong to; violating it is speciesism, which isn't limited to exploitation, to total disregard, or to anthropocentrism.

    6 min
  6. 6
    Could Questions of Desert Justify Speciesism?

    Desert can sometimes change priorities, but only for accountable agents who have broken some rule; since animals aren't accountable, desert doesn't justify giving them less weight.

    3 min
  7. 7
    Is the Rejection of Speciesism Compatible with Only One View of Ethics?

    Rejecting speciesism doesn't require signing on to a specific ethical theory, like utilitarianism: a wide range of theories that disagree about a great deal can agree in condemning the exploitation of animals.

    3 min
  8. 8
    If We Reject Speciesism, How Will We Decide Questions of Priority?

    Giving equal consideration to all sentient beings doesn't leave us without guidance for setting priorities: there are several criteria compatible with it, such as prioritizing those who are worse off, the greater number, the greater benefit, or the greater future impact.

    2 min
  9. 9
    But Aren't Humans Capable of Suffering to a Greater Degree?

    More complex cognition doesn't always mean suffering more: sometimes it increases suffering (anticipation), sometimes it reduces it (understanding that a treatment is for your own good); and lesser cognition usually comes with greater vulnerability.

    1 min
  10. 10
    What Would We Decide Under Conditions of Impartiality?

    A decision is fair if it's the one we would choose without knowing which position we'd occupy among those affected; not knowing whether we'd be human or animal, we wouldn't accept exploitation or speciesism.

    9 min
  11. 11
    Testing Whether an Attitude Is Speciesist

    An attitude is speciesist if it's unfair to sentient beings because of their species; we can test it by asking whether we'd keep the decision without knowing the species of those affected, without knowing our own, if the victims were human, or if we switched places.

    2 min
  12. 12
    How Much Importance Should We Give to the Situation of Animals?

    Faced with “why worry about animals when so many humans need help?”, the chapter shows that, by severity, number of victims, and neglect, the animal cause would deserve very high priority under conditions of impartiality.

    1 min
  13. 13
    Uncertainty About Sentience and the Benefit of the Doubt

    When we're uncertain whether a being is sentient, we should give it the benefit of the doubt: err on the side of caution, because treating it as an object, if it does feel, is a more serious mistake than the reverse.

    10 min
