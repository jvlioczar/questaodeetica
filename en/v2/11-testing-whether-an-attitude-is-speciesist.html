Before you read study layer, not part of the original text

An attitude is speciesist if it's unfair to sentient beings because of their species; we can test it by asking whether we'd keep the decision without knowing the species of those affected, without knowing our own, if the victims were human, or if we switched places.

One way to find out whether we're being unfair: swap the characters in the story. If something only seems acceptable because the victim is an animal, and it wouldn't be if it were a person feeling the same thing, then we're being unfair to the animal.

Reviewers of scientific papers sometimes evaluate them without knowing the author's name, so as not to favor friends. The speciesism test is evaluating without knowing the species, so as not to favor our own.

  1. P1 An attitude is speciesist if it treats sentient beings worse because of their species.
  2. P2 If the decision would change just by changing the species of those affected, or our own position, then species is weighing on the decision.
  3. P3 The practices that affect animals don't pass this test.
  4. C Therefore, besides being unjustified, these practices call on us to do something to change them.

The chapter’s central argument in logical form. If the premises are true, the conclusion follows. To disagree, you have to show which premise is false.

They say “I'm not speciesist, I love animals.”

Answer Liking animals isn't enough. The test is about decisions: would you keep every decision that affects animals if the victims were human, with harms of the same size?

Original text Luciano Carlos Cunha · Volume II, p. 39 of the PDF edition · English translation

An attitude is speciesist if it is unjust toward beings who do not belong to a certain species (or species) — as, for example, when it is unjust toward those who are not human — or who do belong to a certain species (or species) — as, for example, when it is unjust toward animals classified as members of invasive species.

There are several ways to test whether an attitude is unjust. One is to check whether it violates the principle of equal consideration. Another is to check whether it passes the impartiality test.

Testing whether or not an attitude is may be simpler than we imagine. There are some questions47 we can always ask to test whether we are being unjust toward nonhuman animals.

For example, we can ask whether we would consider the attitude in question just:

(1) If we did not know the species of those who would be harmed and those who would be benefited by it;

(2) If we did not know what species we belong to;

(3) If its victims were human, suffering harms of the same magnitude;

(4) If the roles were reversed (for example, if the beneficiaries were nonhuman animals and those harmed were humans);

(5) If we had to bear the harms it causes in order to obtain the benefits it provides;

(6) If we had to bear the harms it causes so that other individuals could obtain the benefits it provides.

If we asked these questions sincerely, it seems we would reach the following conclusions:

(1) Animal exploitation would be widely considered unjust, or even heinous.

(2) It also seems that we would reject giving differentiated degrees of depending on the species a sentient being belongs to or on its capacities. That is, we would reject speciesism.

(3) It seems that we would consider unjust not only practices that involve actively harming nonhuman animals, but also the failure to help them when they are victims of 48 such as hunger, thirst, disease, natural disasters, and so on (which is what commonly happens to wild animals).

All of this seems to indicate not only that there is no justification for what is done to nonhuman animals, but also that we should do something to try to change this situation.

How can we test whether we are being biased with regard to animals? (II, ch. 11)
We can ask whether we would keep the same decision: (1) if we did not know the species of those affected; (2) if we did not know the species to which we belong; (3) if the victims were human; (4) if we had to bear the harms it causes in order to obtain the benefits it brings (or so that other individuals obtain these benefits).
